# Nira and the Flicker-Bird

The bird changed direction six times before Nira finished tying one basket.

It was a small orchard bird, quick enough that its pale wing edges seemed to appear and disappear between the trees.

Usually it crossed the upper garden in one clean line.

Today it darted toward the pear rows, turned sharply, rose, dropped, and turned again.

Nira watched from the packing bench.

“Hawk,” she said.

Sella looked up. “Where?”

“I do not know yet.”

That was the first problem with Nira's answer.

If she did not know where the hawk was, she did not know there was a hawk.

The flicker-bird turned again.

Nira left the basket and walked to the edge of the row.

She checked the sky.

No circling bird. No alarm calls from the other trees. No sudden silence in the hedge.

The flicker-bird dropped low, then climbed steeply beside the third pear tree.

Not away from the sky.

Away from something near the ground.

Nira crouched.

At first she saw only leaves, grass and the thin support cords used around the young trees.

Then the wind moved one strand that should not have been there.

A length of old fruit netting had come loose from its storage bundle and stretched between a low branch and a garden stake. Most of it lay in the grass. One nearly invisible section hung across the narrow working path.

The flicker-bird approached again, saw it late and twisted aside.

Nira stood.

“Not a hawk.”

Sella came over.

“What then?”

Nira pointed without touching the net.

Sella stepped closer and finally saw it.

“Oh.”

Maer Iven was bringing two empty barrows down the row.

Nira raised a hand.

“Stop there. Loose net across the path.”

Iven stopped and looked.

“I can barely see it.”

“Neither could the bird.”

They marked the path from both ends while Iven gathered the loose netting and checked the rest of the stored bundle.

One section had snagged beneath a crate. When the wind pulled the loose end, the net tightened across the path and then sagged again, which explained why Nira had not noticed it before the bird changed course.

Sella leaned against the packing bench.

“So your bird warning worked.”

“My hawk warning did not.”

“You did not actually warn anyone about a hawk.”

“I nearly did.”

Iven carried the bundled net to a closed storage hook.

“Nearly is why checking exists,” he said.

Nira looked at the sky one more time.

Still no hawk.

The flicker-bird settled on the far hedge.

It preened one wing as if none of this concerned it.

Later that afternoon, the same bird began darting again near the bean beds.

Sella noticed first.

“Nira. Another invisible disaster?”

Nira looked at the bird.

It dropped, turned and chased a cloud of tiny insects above the warm soil.

“No,” she said.

“How do you know?”

“I do not yet.”

They watched for another minute.

The bird caught an insect and returned for another.

Other small birds joined it.

No one avoided the ground. Nothing near the beans moved except insects and leaves.

Nira picked up her basket.

“This time it is probably lunch.”

Sella smiled.

“Less dramatic.”

“Better checked.”

The next morning, Sella tried to turn the bird into a system.

She had drawn three little shapes on a scrap of card.

“Straight flight means safe,” she said. “Sharp turns mean danger. Low flight means something on the ground.”

Nira looked at the card.

“That is too neat.”

“It is based on evidence.”

“It is based on two afternoons.”

Sella lowered the card slightly.

“Fine. Based on some evidence.”

They carried it into the orchard anyway, not as a rule but as something to test.

The flicker-bird appeared near the plum trees. It flew straight for several seconds.

“Safe,” Sella said.

A gardener immediately stepped around a bucket left in the path.

Sella crossed out **straight = safe**.

Then the bird made three sharp turns around a branch covered in berries.

No net. No predator. No hazard.

It landed and ate.

Sella crossed out **sharp = danger**.

Nira was trying not to smile.

“You may enjoy this too much,” Sella said.

“I enjoy cards becoming more accurate.”

“What is left?”

Nira took the charcoal.

She wrote:

**UNUSUAL MOVEMENT MAY BE WORTH LOOKING AT. MOVEMENT DOES NOT EXPLAIN ITSELF.**

Sella read the sentence.

“That is a terrible bird code.”

“It is not a code.”

“Exactly.”

Around midday a second flicker-bird struck the orchard air with a sudden sideways turn and gave a short alarm call.

This time Nira's stomach tightened before she had any explanation.

She checked the sky.

Nothing.

She checked the ground.

Nothing.

Then Sella pointed into the hedge.

A ginger farm cat crouched beneath the leaves.

The bird's alarm made sense now.

Iven called the cat away from the nesting hedge and closed the small gate behind it.

Sella held up the amended card.

“Danger?”

“Risk to the bird,” Nira said. “Not necessarily to us.”

Sella added another note.

The following morning, the flicker-bird gave the same sharp alarm while Nira was alone at the packing bench.

She checked the hedge for the cat.

Nothing.

She checked the path for netting.

Nothing.

A larger bird crossed high above the orchard and vanished beyond the roofline before Nira could identify it.

The flicker-bird settled again almost immediately.

Nira wrote one line on Sella's card:

**cause not established**.

When Sella arrived, she read it and asked, “You did not solve this one?”

“No.”

“Does that bother you?”

“Yes.”

They left the line anyway. An unexplained observation was better than a confident story pasted over a gap.

By the end of the day the card had more crossed-out certainty than useful rules.

Nira liked it better that way.

The flicker-bird's movements could draw attention to something changing around it. Sometimes that something was loose netting. Sometimes insects. Sometimes a cat. Sometimes, probably, nothing Nira would ever understand.

The useful part was not treating the bird as a messenger sent to her.

It was noticing a pattern, making a possible explanation, and then looking at the world closely enough to let the explanation fail.

The bird flickered once more across the beans.

Nira watched it long enough to see an insect disappear into its beak.

Then she went back to tying baskets.
