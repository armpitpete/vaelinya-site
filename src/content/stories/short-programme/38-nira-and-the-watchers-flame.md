# Nira and the Watcher's Flame

The light on the far hill blinked twice.

Nira noticed because it had been steady for most of the evening.

She was sitting inside the east watch room with Maer Iven, sorting route tallies while he checked the valley path through the window. The distant light belonged to another staffed watch post across the valley. Nira was not responsible for its code and did not touch the signalling equipment in this room.

The light blinked again.

Twice.

Then once.

Nira put down the tallies.

“Was that deliberate?”

Iven looked up.

“Maybe. Watch the timing.”

They did.

Two short dark gaps.

One longer gap.

Then steady light.

Nira looked at the local signal sheet fixed beside the window.

“Two-one is listed as route question.”

“In this district,” Iven said.

“Not automatically theirs?”

“Correct.”

That was irritatingly sensible.

The two watch posts could see one another, but they did not invent meanings on the spot. Their adult signal keepers shared a written set of agreed patterns for ordinary route questions, acknowledgements and urgent closures. Nira had seen the sheet before. Seeing a familiar number did not mean she was authorised to answer it.

A minute later the pattern repeated.

Two short dark gaps.

One long.

Iven checked the posted cross-station notes, then asked the adult signal keeper in the adjoining room to confirm the shared pattern.

The keeper, Maer Tessa, came in, watched one full repetition and nodded.

“Route question. They are asking whether the lower east crossing remains open.”

Nira looked at the tally board.

A cart team had reported that crossing clear less than an hour ago.

“Open,” she said.

Then she stopped herself.

“That answer might be stale.”

Iven smiled slightly.

“What would you check?”

The last tally had a time mark beside it. Since then, a repair crew had gone down toward the crossing with timber and tools. Nira could not tell from the room whether they had blocked part of the route.

“We need something current.”

Tessa nodded.

They did not answer from memory.

Iven sent an adult runner to the nearer checkpoint and waited for confirmation. While they waited, Nira kept watching the far light.

Once, it seemed to blink three times.

She marked it on scrap paper.

The next cycle returned to two-one.

“Was the three a new message?” she asked.

Tessa shook her head. “Do not decide yet. Watch the light and the sky together.”

A thin cloud edge moved across the hill.

For a moment the flame dimmed, disappeared, returned, then dimmed again.

Nira compared that irregular rhythm with the deliberate signal.

The cloud did not make clean dark gaps. It softened the whole glow as it passed.

“Environmental interruption,” Nira said.

“Likely,” Tessa replied.

“Not communication.”

“Not unless the station repeats it deliberately.”

The far post did not.

Nira crossed out her three-mark note and rewrote it:

**possible cloud interruption — not counted as signal**.

The runner had not returned yet.

Nira found waiting harder than noticing.

The question was visible. The old answer was visible. The signalling equipment sat only a few steps away.

They could have sent **open** and corrected it later if necessary.

Tessa seemed to read the temptation from Nira's face.

“What is the cost of waiting?” she asked.

“The far post waits longer.”

“And the cost of sending old information as current?”

Nira looked at the tally board.

“Someone may use the crossing because we told them it is open now.”

They waited.

The far post repeated two-one again.

Tessa sent the agreed **received, checking** pattern rather than an answer about the route.

Nira liked that. Silence did not have to pretend certainty. There was a way to communicate that the question had been heard without making up the missing fact.

The adult runner returned at last, breathing hard from the climb.

“Crossing open,” she said. “One side narrowed. Repair crew has a marked passage. Handcarts only until they finish lifting the rail.”

Iven wrote the current report.

Tessa checked the wording against the signal sheet. The code could answer **open with restriction**, but the exact restriction was too detailed for the simple lamp pattern.

“So how do they learn the rest?” Nira asked.

“Messenger at the next route handover,” Iven said. “The light tells them enough not to treat the crossing as closed or fully ordinary.”

The adults sent the agreed reply.

Nira watched the far post acknowledge it.

One short dark gap.

Steady.

Tessa recorded the exchange in the station log: question received, current route check requested, restricted-open reply sent, acknowledgement received.

Nothing about the light interpreted itself.

Its meaning came from the shared convention, the current information and the people responsible for using both.

Later, while Nira sorted the last tallies, the far light vanished completely.

She looked up at once.

No regular gap.

No return.

“Tessa?”

The signal keeper came to the window.

Before either of them did anything, the flame reappeared.

A dark human shape moved beside it.

“Someone passed in front of the lamp,” Nira said.

“Probably.”

They watched.

The light remained steady.

No code followed.

Nira added nothing to the log.

The next evening, Iven gave Nira yesterday's scrap notes and asked her to sort them into three piles.

**signal**

**possible interruption**

**current route evidence**

The deliberate two-one pattern went into signal.

The cloud dimming and the person crossing the lamp went into interruption.

The runner's crossing report went into route evidence.

Nira looked at the three piles.

“They all mattered to the same exchange.”

“Yes,” Iven said.

“But they were not the same kind of thing.”

“No.”

That distinction pleased her.

A repeating light could be communication.

A cloud could imitate part of a pattern.

A known code could still carry an answer that was out of date.

A correct interpretation of the signal did not automatically make the underlying fact correct.

The far light blinked once that evening.

Nira looked at Tessa.

Tessa looked at the shared sheet.

“Ordinary acknowledgement from another exchange,” she said.

Nira went back to the tallies.

She no longer needed every flicker to become a message for her.
