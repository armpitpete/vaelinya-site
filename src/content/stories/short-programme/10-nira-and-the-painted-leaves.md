# Nira and the Painted Leaves

The leaves were painted orange on one side.

That was Yara's explanation.

Nira crouched beside the lower growing bed and rubbed one mark with her thumb. Fine grit came away.

“Clay,” Yara said.

“Probably.”

“It is orange.”

“Orange is a colour, not a cause.”

Yara folded her arms. “You say things like that because you enjoy making simple answers wait outside.”

Nira looked at the leaf again.

“Sometimes they deserve to come in.”

The marks had appeared after the night rain. Most beds were merely wet. This one shone with too much water, and several young bean stems leaned against their supports as if the soil had loosened around them.

Maer Delen had asked Nira and Yara to check whether the water was coming from the upper path or from the irrigation channel. He had not asked them to solve it alone. He was two beds away, moving trays out of the soggiest ground while they looked.

They followed the marked leaves uphill.

Near the top, each leaf carried a few dots. Lower down, the dots became streaks. At the bottom corner, broad leaves were coated along one edge.

“No message,” Nira said.

“I did not say message.”

“You said painted.”

Yara sighed.

Nira studied the streaks. They were long on one side and sharp on the other.

“Splash came from there.”

She pointed across the bed, not uphill.

Yara looked where she pointed. “That is not where the water is deepest.”

“No. It may be where it entered.”

They followed the direction to a strip of grass beside a retaining wall. Nothing there looked unusual.

Nira pressed her boot down.

Water rose around the sole.

Two steps higher, the grass was firmer. Two steps lower, it was wet again.

Yara knelt and touched the muddy water with one finger.

“Orange.”

“Still a colour.”

“Very persistent colour.”

They fetched Maer Delen.

He brought a narrow probe and pushed it into the ground. At one point it struck stone. At another it sank easily and came back coated in the same orange clay.

“There used to be an overflow run under here,” he said. “It should be dry except after very heavy rain.”

“Should be,” Nira repeated.

Delen smiled. “Yes.”

Yara pointed toward the growing bed. “Then the old run is leaking.”

“Maybe,” Nira said.

Yara made an injured sound.

Delen laughed. “She is right. We have a good idea, not a confirmed route.”

They did not dig immediately.

First they tested.

Delen poured a bucket into the upper drainage channel. Nira watched the wet grass.

Nothing.

Yara folded her arms. “Wrong theory.”

A bubble appeared beside a clay-stained stone in the channel wall.

Nira pointed.

“Again.”

They refilled the bucket. This time Nira laid a dry scrap of pale cloth on the wet strip.

Delen poured.

For several breaths, nothing happened.

Then one corner of the cloth darkened from below.

Yara knelt beside it.

“Connected.”

“Somewhere.”

They tried once more, this time placing two cloth scraps farther apart. The nearer one darkened first. The farther one stayed dry.

“That gives us a smaller place to open,” Delen said.

They lifted a narrow strip of turf.

Beneath it lay the old overflow run: two shallow stone sides with a clay bed. One side had fallen inward. Washed soil partly blocked the upper end.

During heavy rain, water entered faster than it could leave. It pressed through the damaged side beneath the grass and sprayed across the growing bed.

The leaves had not been writing instructions.

They had simply been in the way of the spray.

Delen cleared the blockage and reset two stones. Yara carried the washed soil away in a shallow tray while Nira compared the clay from the broken run with the grit she had rubbed from the leaves.

Same colour.

Same fine texture.

Still not magic.

Then they tested again.

Water passed through the old run.

The cloth stayed dry.

“Fixed,” Yara said.

“The test passed once.”

Yara groaned.

They tested twice more.

The cloth remained dry.

By afternoon the lower bed had stopped gaining water. Delen loosened the soil carefully around the leaning bean stems and added dry compost to the worst patch.

Nira wiped one orange leaf clean.

“Should we wash the rest?” Yara asked.

Nira looked at the harmless grit.

“They can be orange until the rain removes it.”

The next morning, a light shower had done exactly that.

Nira checked the grass beside the wall.

Dry.

Yara arrived carrying two empty cloth scraps.

“You are checking again.”

“Yes.”

“I brought evidence because I know you now.”

They placed the scraps over the repaired strip and poured one more bucket into the upper channel.

Nothing rose through the soil.

Yara lifted both pieces dramatically.

“Dry. May the simple answer enter the room?”

“It can sit near the door.”

Yara rolled her eyes.

Three days later they found another orange mark on a leaf near the opposite end of the garden.

Yara called Nira over.

“Same problem?”

Nira crouched.

The mark was powdery and dry. No nearby ground was wet. A stack of clay planting pots stood upwind, one chipped at the rim.

Nira rubbed the leaf.

A little dust came away.

Yara looked from the mark to the pots.

“Different cause.”

“Probably.”

This time Yara said it first.

They brushed the leaf clean and went back to work.

A week later, heavier rain came during the night. Nira reached the garden early enough to see drops still hanging from the bean strings.

Yara was already beside the repaired wall.

“No orange leaves,” she said.

Nira pressed one boot into the grass. Firm.

They checked the lower bed. Wet from rain, but not flooded. The repaired run carried a thin stream toward the outlet exactly where Delen had intended it to go.

Yara looked almost disappointed.

“So the evidence today is mostly that the old evidence is missing.”

“And that the water is going somewhere else.”

“Can absence be evidence?”

“Only if we expected the thing to be there.”

Yara looked at the clean leaves and then at the working drain.

“That answer may enter the room.”

Nira smiled.

The leaves had never contained a secret message.

They had contained mud, and later dust.

The first mud marks had contained a direction only because water, clay, slope and splash had left a pattern that could be checked against the ground.

That was useful enough.
