# Fina and the Drum of First Light

The square was ready for a beginning that nobody had agreed how to begin.

Before sunrise, people had carried everything out of the storehouse and stopped.

Boards lay beside the long table. Four poles for the shade frame rested against the wall. Chalk sat in a blue bowl. Children had brought cups, string and bundles of paper signs. Someone had even carried out the old festival drum and set it under the awning where the first light could reach its copper rim.

The square looked busy.

Nobody in it was doing anything.

Fina stood on one foot, then the other.

“Are we waiting for something?” she asked.

Taren, who was coordinating the work, looked down at the plan in their hands.

“We still do not know the final layout.”

Fina leaned sideways to look at the page. Lines crossed lines. Someone had drawn the table near the doorway, then crossed it out. The shade frame had three possible positions. Small arrows showed where people might walk, except the arrows ran through one another.

“We know where the table goes,” Fina said.

“We think we know.”

“We know it cannot go in the doorway.”

“That part is true.”

“And it cannot block the water point.”

“Also true.”

Fina looked across the empty square.

Those were two real things. The page contained twenty things nobody had settled.

She bounced once on the balls of her feet.

“What is the smallest thing we can do that does not decide everything else?”

Taren lowered the plan.

Around them, two adults were discussing the shade cloth. Mara had brought a low delivery cart full of cups. A pair of younger children had begun tying and untying the same piece of string because they had been told to wait.

Taren looked at the chalk.

“Mark the table corners,” they said. “Chalk only. Nothing fixed.”

Fina took four pieces before anyone could change their mind.

The festival drum caught her eye.

Its painted sides showed the pale red lines of first light over the hills. Usually it was sounded when festival work began. Fina had been waiting all week to strike it.

She reached for the beater.

Then stopped.

“Does the drum start the work,” she asked, “or does it tell people the work has started?”

Taren’s mouth twitched.

“Today? I think the second one.”

Fina put the beater down.

“Fine.”

She ran to the square.

The first chalk corner went beside the doorway, leaving a wide gap.

The second sat near the wall.

The third lined up with the edge of the water point.

Fina crouched at the fourth place.

“Mara?” called Taren.

Too late.

Mara was already pushing the delivery cart through the doorway.

“Coming through.”

Fina jumped aside.

The cart cleared the first chalk mark, rolled past the second and then needed to turn toward the store tables. Its back wheel crossed exactly where Fina was about to draw the fourth corner.

Mara stopped.

“That is tighter than it looked on the page.”

Fina stared at the wheel track.

“So the first three marks are wrong?”

“Not necessarily,” said Taren. “But they are provisional.”

Fina rubbed one mark away with the side of her shoe.

The chalk smeared into a pale cloud.

For a moment, she wanted to move all four marks at once. Her body was already halfway into the decision before her thoughts caught up.

“Wait,” Mara said. “Try the cart again first.”

Fina stopped.

Mara backed out.

This time Fina walked beside the cart, watching the wheels instead of the doorway. Two children stood where the table edge would be and held a length of string between them.

“Wider,” Mara called.

They moved.

“Again.”

They moved again.

On the third turn, the cart passed without touching the string.

Fina drew a new corner.

“There.”

“Maybe,” said Mara.

Fina grinned. “Maybe there.”

That was enough to make other people move.

Seli and Oran lifted one shade pole and held it upright.

Someone placed the paper signs on two baskets and carried them to the path entrances.

The younger children stopped untying their string and became extremely serious sign-testers.

“I can see that one!” one shouted from the east path.

“I cannot!” called the other from the south.

Fina ran between them.

The sign that was clear from the east disappeared behind the shade pole from the south.

“Move the pole,” Fina said.

“Or move the sign,” said Seli.

Fina opened her mouth, shut it, and looked.

The pole mattered because it would hold the shade. The sign mattered because people needed to find the food table.

Neither was more important just because Fina had noticed one first.

They tried moving the sign.

That worked from both paths.

Then the sun came over the roofs.

A bright strip of light reached across the square and landed exactly where the table was marked.

Oran squinted.

“If the table stays there, the food will be in full sun by midday.”

Fina looked at Taren.

Taren looked at the plan.

The plan did not know where the morning light had fallen.

“Move it?” Fina asked.

“Test the shade first,” said Taren.

Fina made an impatient noise.

The shade cloth was still folded.

Testing it required untying the bundle, lifting two poles, asking four people to hold corners and waiting while Seli found the missing rope.

Fina paced around the chalk rectangle.

The drum waited under the awning.

The square smelled of dust, bread from the nearby kitchen, and the faint sharp smell of morning stone warming under sunlight.

At last the cloth rose.

For three breaths it made a perfect patch of shade over the chalk table.

Then a gust came through the south path.

The cloth snapped like a sail.

One pole tilted.

“Down!” Taren called.

Everyone lowered it.

No one was hurt, but the cloth ended up in a heap and one of the children sat down hard on the ground.

Fina stared at the fallen pole.

“Well,” she said.

Mara laughed.

“Well is right.”

The wind came again, smaller this time. A line of paper signs fluttered toward the wall.

Taren crouched beside the plan and drew a new arrow.

“We know something else now.”

“The wind is annoying?”

“We knew that already.”

Fina laughed.

“The south path channels it.”

“Yes.”

Oran tapped a different place on the plan.

“If we turn the shade frame this way, the broad side will not catch as much.”

“And the table?” Fina asked.

“Move it half a length west,” Mara said. “The cart can still turn.”

They tested that.

The cart turned.

The signs stayed visible.

The shade cloth lifted without pulling hard against the poles.

The water point stayed clear.

Fina drew new corners, slower this time.

She did not enjoy slower as much as faster.

But she liked seeing the square change from a blank place into something people could actually use.

By the time breakfast arrived, the chalk had been rubbed out and redrawn so often that pale ghosts of possible tables covered the stones.

The final rectangle was not where Fina had first put it.

The shade frame was not where Taren’s plan had shown it.

One sign hung from the wall instead of a pole.

And the west side of the square was still empty because nobody yet knew whether another workbench would be needed.

“That part can wait,” Taren said.

Fina looked at the empty space.

Earlier it would have bothered her.

Now it looked less like a failure to decide and more like room for something they did not know yet.

Mara handed her a cup of water.

“You have chalk on your nose.”

Fina wiped the wrong side of her face.

“Still there,” Mara said.

Fina wiped again.

The younger children had begun laying cups on the table. Seli tightened the last shade rope. Oran walked both path entrances one more time and lifted a hand.

“Signs are clear.”

Taren folded the plan.

Fina’s eyes went to the drum.

“Now?”

Taren looked around the square.

Not at Fina.

At everyone.

“Is everyone ready to call this started?”

Mara raised a hand.

Seli did too.

The children shouted yes before being asked.

Oran pointed at the empty west side. “Started. Not finished.”

“Good,” said Taren.

Fina took the drum beater.

The copper rim was warm now.

She struck once.

The sound rolled across the square, deep enough to feel in her ribs. People at the kitchen door looked up. Someone on the far path answered with a cheer.

Fina held the beater still and listened until the note faded.

Then Mara pushed the first full cart through the doorway.

It turned cleanly past the table.

Fina ran beside it, not because she had solved the square, but because there was finally somewhere useful to go.
