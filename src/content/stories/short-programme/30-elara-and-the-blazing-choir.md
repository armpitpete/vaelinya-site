# Elara and the Blazing Choir

The choir sounded beautiful from the courtyard.

Inside the hall, it was too much for Elara.

Thirty voices rose beneath the timber roof while hand drums kept a fast festival pulse. The sound pressed against the walls and returned from the ceiling. Some singers loved the size of it. A few people near the side wall had stopped singing.

Elara tried once more to join the next chorus.

She knew the melody. She liked the melody.

The drums began.

The first row came in strongly, the second row answered, and then everyone reached the high line together.

Elara's shoulders tightened before she could stop them.

She stepped outside.

Talla followed.

“Too much?” she asked.

“For me.”

“For me too.”

That did not make it true for everyone.

Through the open door, Elara could see Bram in the centre row grinning so widely he nearly missed his next entrance. Two drummers were laughing between phrases. On the far side, Mira had both hands over one ear but was still singing with the other hand holding her music card.

The song ended.

Maer Evin came to the door.

“We can make the whole choir quieter.”

From inside, someone called, “Please do not.”

Several people laughed.

Elara did too.

Bram leaned around the door frame.

“The big chorus is the best part.”

Mira, behind him, said, “For you.”

“Correct,” Bram said cheerfully.

Evin rubbed the bridge of his nose.

“I was hoping for one adjustment.”

“What if quiet is not the only adjustment?” Elara asked.

They tested the room before changing the music.

The centre was strongest. Near the open side doors, the sound was softer because some of it escaped into the courtyard. Behind a rear cloth screen, voices remained clear but the drums lost their sharp edge. Near the high windows, the roof reflected the upper notes more strongly.

Talla preferred the screen.

One singer tried it and came straight back out.

“I hate hearing everyone through cloth.”

“Then do not stand there,” Talla said.

Bram moved from the centre to the doorway and sang one verse.

“This feels thin.”

Elara stood where he had been.

“This feels enormous.”

They swapped back.

Neither position had become correct by vote.

Evin moved the drum line to the far end of the hall. That helped the side wall and made the rear singers complain that the beat had become harder to follow.

They moved half the drums back.

Then the choir split one chorus into two answering groups instead of everyone singing at once.

Some people loved the extra space in the sound.

Others missed the full wall of voices.

One drummer said the answering version felt as if the song had lost its feet.

Talla said she could finally hear the words.

Evin rubbed his forehead again.

“We are producing many correct answers.”

Elara smiled. “That may be the problem with asking for one.”

They kept three rehearsal arrangements: full choir, answering choir and open-door choir. Nobody had to stay in one place for the whole session.

The first time they tried that freedom, it became messy.

Three singers moved at once during a verse and bumped into a bench. Another person crossed in front of the drummers and had to duck around a raised hand drum.

Evin stopped them.

“Moving is allowed. Collisions are not.”

They marked two clear side routes with pale cord and left the central performance space free. People could step out between verses, move to the courtyard, or return through the side doors without crossing the drum line.

That was not an emotional rule.

It was a floor plan.

During the next full chorus, Elara stood near the door. When she had enough, she waited for the verse to finish, stepped into the courtyard and kept listening from there.

No one followed her to persuade her back inside.

The song continued without treating her exit as criticism.

When the answering chorus began, she returned.

This time Talla stayed outside.

“You are missing your preferred one,” Elara said through the doorway.

“I know. I am tired now.”

Talla's limit had changed in less than an hour.

Later, Bram came outside too.

Elara looked at him.

“What happened to the best part?”

“It is still the best part,” he said. “My head is full.”

He drank water, missed one full song by choice, then went back in for the final rehearsal.

That mattered to Elara. A person could love intensity and still reach a limit. Another person could dislike the same intensity from the first note. Neither needed to become the example everybody else was measured against.

The festival performance used all three arrangements.

The first song began with the answering groups, one on either side of the hall. The second opened the side doors and let the chorus spill into the courtyard. The final song used the full choir and every drum that wanted to play.

Before it began, Evin reminded the audience that the courtyard remained part of the performance space and the side routes would stay clear.

Nobody called them quiet zones or sensitive zones.

They were places from which the music could be experienced differently.

During the full song, Mira moved behind the cloth screen. Talla went outside. Bram stayed in the centre through the first two verses, then joined Elara at the door for the last one.

A younger child covered both ears and danced in the courtyard anyway.

An older woman stood directly beside the open doorway and sang louder than anyone inside.

The choir did not become quieter as a whole.

It became less demanding about where participation had to happen.

Afterward, Evin found Elara stacking benches.

“Did we solve it?”

One drummer was still talking excitedly about the full chorus. Talla was sitting quietly outside. Two singers were comparing where they had stood for each song. Mira had decided she preferred the screen only when drums were playing. Bram was trying to persuade someone to repeat the final verse in the courtyard.

“No,” Elara said. “We made more than one way to stay part of it.”

Evin looked at the marked side route.

“And next rehearsal?”

“Ask again.”

“Of course.”

A last drumbeat sounded from the courtyard, followed immediately by someone calling, “Not beside my head, please.”

The drummer moved two steps away.

The person who had asked stayed where they were.

Elara pointed toward the door.

“They are still tuning it.”
