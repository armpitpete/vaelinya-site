# Nira and the Silver Fibres

The first bright fibre was stuck to Nira’s sleeve.

She noticed it when the morning light reached across the work table and made one tiny strand flash against the brown cloth.

Nira pinched it between two fingers.

It was finer than sewing thread and almost colourless until she turned it. Then it shone silver.

Jori leaned over.

“Treasure.”

“It is fluff.”

“Shining treasure fluff.”

Nira laid it on a dark piece of paper.

The workroom was used by three different groups that week. One table held baskets of rope and cord waiting to be checked. Another held folded cloth from the market awnings. The third was covered with wooden tags, ink and bundles of labels.

Nothing seemed to be missing.

Nira would have forgotten the fibre if she had not found another one after lunch.

This one lay on the rope table.

The next morning there was a third on the label table.

Jori saw that one first.

“Now it is a trail.”

Nira looked from one table to the next.

The three fibres made a rough line through the room.

Jori pointed toward the door.

“Maybe something passed through here and left them behind.”

“Maybe.”

“Or something is being pulled apart.”

“Maybe.”

“Or they are showing us where to go.”

Nira looked at him.

“Fibres do not know where we need to go.”

“You know what I mean.”

Nira did.

A line was tempting.

Three bright marks in three places made the mind want a path, especially when the marks were unusual.

She knew that feeling. A pattern could become a story very quickly.

She put all three fibres on the dark paper instead.

“Before we follow where they are,” she said, “we should check what they are.”

Jori sighed as if Nira had ruined a very good mystery.

They compared the strands.

All three had the same twist.

One had a tiny blue mark at one end.

Nira held it close to the window.

“Dye?”

Jori squinted.

“Paint?”

“Could be.”

They checked the rope baskets first.

There were red cords, brown cords, pale plant-fibre cords and one silver-grey line used for measuring.

The measuring line looked promising.

Jori held it up triumphantly.

“Found it.”

Nira compared the loose strand with the cord.

The colour was close.

The twist was not.

The measuring cord had three thick fibres wound together. The loose strands were made of many much finer threads.

Jori lowered the cord.

“Not found it.”

“Not that.”

They checked the knots and cut ends anyway.

Nothing was fraying.

At the cloth table, the market awnings were folded into heavy squares.

One had blue stripes.

Jori pointed.

“There.”

They unfolded it.

The blue was too dark, and the cloth fibres were soft and fuzzy instead of smooth.

Another wrong answer.

By then the workroom keeper, Maer Pell, had noticed what they were doing.

“What have you two lost?”

“Nothing,” said Nira.

“We found something,” said Jori.

“That sounds more expensive.”

Nira showed the three fibres.

Pell rubbed one gently between finger and thumb.

“Fine weave.”

“Do you know where from?”

“No.”

Jori looked pleased. Adults not knowing made the mystery feel larger.

Pell did not seem bothered.

“Where did you find them?”

Nira showed the three tables.

Pell followed the line with their eyes.

“It does look like something moved through.”

“That is what I said,” Jori told Nira.

“It looks like it,” Nira said.

Pell nodded. “Looks like is useful. It is not the same as is.”

They checked what had moved between the tables.

A basket of labels had gone from the door to the rope table.

A bundle of awning ties had gone from the cloth table to the store shelf.

Pell had carried a folded curtain through the room the previous afternoon.

That sounded promising.

They found the curtain.

No silver fibres.

Nira went back to the tables.

The first fibre had been on her sleeve.

She tried to remember where she had been before she noticed it.

The doorway.

The bench.

The rope table.

The water jug.

Everywhere.

“Too many places,” she said.

Jori had begun holding the dark paper at different angles, making the fibres flash.

“Maybe the blue bit matters more.”

Nira looked up.

The tiny blue mark had seemed like a flaw.

Now it was the only part of the evidence that was not almost colourless.

They searched for pale material with blue edging.

The rope baskets had none.

The awnings had broad blue stripes, not an edge.

A stack of work cloths had blue stitching, but the fibres were thick.

Nira stood in the middle of the room and turned slowly.

Ropes.

Cloth.

Labels.

Shelves.

Window.

Above the tables hung a dust cover rolled around a wooden bar. It had been tied up because people were working beneath it.

Along its bottom edge ran a faded blue band.

Nira pointed.

Pell brought the cover down.

At first it looked intact.

Then they spread it over the table.

One corner had rubbed against a rough iron hook in the wall.

The blue edge there was frayed.

Pell teased out one loose strand and laid it beside Nira’s fibres.

Same shine.

Same fine twist.

Same pale blue mark.

Jori leaned so close his nose nearly touched the paper.

“So the fibres came from the cover.”

“Probably,” Nira said.

“Probably?”

“We matched the material. We still need to explain how they got onto three tables.”

Pell looked up at the hook.

“Every morning I raise that cover.”

They tried it.

Pell rolled the cloth upward.

The frayed corner brushed the hook.

A bright strand came loose.

The moving cover carried it across the room before the strand floated down near the rope table.

Another caught briefly on Pell’s sleeve.

Nira looked at her own sleeve.

That explained the first one too.

Jori watched a third fibre drift sideways in the light.

“So the line across the tables was not a route.”

“It was where the loose fibres happened to land.”

“That is less exciting.”

Nira was not sure.

A false trail that could be tested was interesting in a different way.

Pell took the cover to the repair bench. An adult cloth worker trimmed the damaged edge, reinforced it and wrapped the iron hook so it would not catch again.

The next morning, Nira checked all three tables.

No bright fibres.

Jori checked under the baskets.

None.

They checked the floor.

Nothing.

“Problem solved,” Jori said.

Nira wrote a short note on the dark paper.

Three matching fibres. Source matched to blue-edged cover. Fraying reproduced at wall hook. Hook wrapped and edge repaired.

Then she added one more line.

No new fibres found the next morning.

Jori read it.

“You wrote down nothing happening.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because nothing happening is different after a change than before we looked.”

Jori considered that.

On the third morning, he came in before Nira.

He was standing over the label table when she arrived.

“There is another silver one.”

Nira stopped.

Jori held up a bright strand.

For one sharp moment, the whole explanation loosened.

They checked the repaired cover.

Still sound.

They checked the wrapped hook.

Still smooth.

Then Nira saw Jori’s grin.

She looked at his sleeve.

A pale measuring cord was tucked into his pocket, and one loose thread hung from it.

“You did that.”

Jori laughed.

“I wanted to see if you would decide too fast.”

Nira took the planted fibre from him and compared it anyway.

Different twist.

“You could at least have chosen a better fake.”

“I am learning.”

Nira placed it on a second piece of paper marked JORI’S VERY POOR FALSE TRAIL.

Jori protested the wording.

Pell, passing the table, read it and laughed.

The repaired cover stayed above them for the rest of the week.

No more matching silver fibres appeared.

The room did not contain a secret path.

It contained cloth, movement, a rough hook, a repair, one mischievous friend and enough small evidence to tell the difference.
