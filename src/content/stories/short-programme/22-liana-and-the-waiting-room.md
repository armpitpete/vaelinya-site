# Liana and the Waiting Room

The path was closed.

That was the whole answer.

It was also not enough.

Rain had loosened the clay bank above the north road before dawn. A section of earth had slipped across the path, carrying stones, roots and half a hawthorn bush with it.

No one knew whether more would fall.

So the path keepers closed the road and went to inspect the slope.

Everyone else waited in the north wayhouse.

Liana sat near the stove with a cup between both hands.

Around her, twenty people tried not to wait.

A trader counted the same six bundles three times.

Two children played a game that involved moving every stool in the room.

An old man opened the door every few minutes to look at the road.

A woman with a covered basket asked anyone entering whether the path had reopened.

No one knew.

At the far wall, Pela sat beside her older sister with her boots tucked under the bench.

Their mother was on the other side of the closure.

“She knows we are here?” Pela asked.

Her sister nodded.

“You said that before.”

“I know.”

“Does she know now?”

Her sister closed her eyes.

Liana moved closer.

“Has anyone sent a message?”

“The path is closed.”

“That was not what I asked.”

The sister looked at her.

A ridge track ran above the north road. It was too steep for carts and not useful for ordinary travel, but one of the path keepers had gone that way to reach the far side of the slip.

Liana found Maer Sera near the door.

“Can the keeper take a message to Pela’s mother?”

Sera thought.

“If he has not left the upper turn yet.”

That was something that could be done.

They wrote the message.

Pela insisted on adding: WE ARE BORED BUT ALIVE.

Her sister added: WE ARE SAFE AT NORTH WAYHOUSE. WAITING FOR ROAD CLEARANCE.

Sera sent a runner up the ridge.

Pela watched until the runner disappeared.

Then she looked at Liana.

“What now?”

“Now we wait.”

Pela groaned.

Liana understood.

Waiting felt worse after action because action made the stillness obvious.

She looked around the wayhouse.

Some things still needed doing.

Wet coats crowded the pegs and dripped onto the floor.

The kettle was nearly empty.

One traveller had no dry socks.

The two stool-moving children had accidentally blocked the door to the wood box.

Liana stood.

“We can wait badly or we can make the room usable.”

Pela’s sister laughed.

“Is that a real choice?”

“Yes.”

They started with the coats.

Then the stools.

Then the kettle.

Liana asked the trader if one bundle could move from the warm wall so the old man had somewhere to sit.

He moved two.

Someone found dry socks.

Someone else cut bread.

Pela took responsibility for telling new arrivals the only accurate news:

“The path is still closed. The keepers are checking it. We do not have a reopening time.”

At first she added, “Probably soon.”

Liana stopped her.

“We do not know that.”

Pela sighed.

Then she used the exact words.

The room settled.

Not into peace.

Into something more breathable.

An hour passed.

Rain tapped the windows.

The old man went to the door again.

Liana joined him.

“You have checked seven times,” she said.

“Eight.”

“Did it help?”

“No.”

“Do you need to leave when the road opens?”

“My daughter is expecting me.”

“Can we send her a message too?”

He looked at her.

“Yes.”

That became another action.

Three more people had messages that could travel by ridge runner.

One did not.

“My brother knows I am coming,” he said. “He will know the road is closed.”

Liana nodded.

No action needed.

That was different from forgetting him.

Near midday, a young man stood abruptly.

“I can get over the slide.”

Maer Sera looked up from the stove.

“No.”

“I crossed worse last winter.”

“The bank is still moving.”

“I am tired of sitting here.”

Liana watched his hands.

He was not confused about the risk.

He hated the waiting.

Sera said, “If you go onto the closure, the keepers will have to stop inspecting the slope to bring you back.”

The young man looked toward the door.

That changed the choice.

He sat down hard.

Liana brought him a mug of water.

“I do not want tea,” he said.

“It is water.”

He took it.

“Thank you.”

They waited.

There were no clever tasks left after that.

The floor was dry.

Messages had gone.

People had food.

The wood box was accessible.

The road remained closed.

Liana sat beside Pela.

“This part is worse,” Pela whispered.

“Yes.”

“What do we do?”

“Nothing that pretends the answer is here.”

Pela leaned against her sister.

Liana listened to rain on the roof.

A room full of people breathed, shifted, coughed, drank and watched the day move.

Time passed because time passes even when no one improves it.

In the middle of the afternoon, boots sounded outside.

Everyone stood.

The door opened.

One of the path keepers entered, clay to his knees.

“The slope has stopped moving,” he said. “We have a narrow walking route open with guides. No carts until tomorrow.”

The room erupted.

Questions.

Bags.

Coats.

Stools moved again.

Pela grabbed Liana’s sleeve.

“My mother?”

The keeper smiled.

“Met the ridge runner. She is waiting at the north marker.”

Pela made a sound too large for the room and ran to find her boots.

Liana helped the old man tie his bundle.

The young man who had wanted to cross alone waited for the guide group without argument.

Outside, the road was brown with fresh earth.

The landslip still covered half the path.

Pela was already ahead with her sister, looking towards the north marker.

The young man waited until the guide called their group.

Liana looked back through the open wayhouse door.

Cups stood by the wash bowl.

A forgotten sock hung beside the stove.

Maer Sera picked it up.

“Whose?”

No one answered.

Sera put it back beside the stove.

Liana smiled and joined the walking line.
