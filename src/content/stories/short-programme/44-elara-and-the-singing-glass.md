# Elara and the Singing Glass

The glass panel sang whenever the room became loud.

It was not meant to sing.

It was a broad sheet of pale green glass set into a wooden frame beside the meeting table, put there years ago to borrow daylight from the courtyard. Most days it did nothing except catch reflections: sleeves passing, hands carrying tools, dust turning in the sun.

Then the workroom became crowded.

Three groups had been asked to share it for a week while rain repairs closed the rooms across the lane. One group mended nets. One sorted small metal parts. One painted signs for the market paths.

By the first afternoon, the room had twelve people, three work tables, two tool baskets, six stools that nobody could agree where to put, and enough conversation to fill every corner.

The glass began to hum.

At first the sound was so faint that only Elara noticed it.

A high note trembled in the panel whenever several voices rose together.

She turned her head.

The note stopped.

Then Tavi called across the room, “Who moved the blue cord?”

Someone answered from the far table.

A chair scraped.

Two people began talking at once.

The glass sang again.

This time everyone heard.

They laughed.

“Listen to that.”

“Do it again.”

Two children clapped close to the panel.

The glass answered with a bright, unpleasant whine.

An adult shooed them away.

For the rest of the afternoon, the panel became a curiosity.

By the second day, it had become an authority.

“The glass says this room is too loud,” said Hessa, moving a basket away from the central table.

“No, it does not,” said Beren. “It only sings when you complain.”

Hessa stared at him.

A second argument began before the first one had finished.

Their voices climbed.

The glass hummed.

Beren pointed at it.

“See? Angry.”

Elara looked up from the bundle of cord she was sorting.

“No.”

The room quietened.

The panel stopped singing.

Beren folded his arms. “No what?”

“The glass does not know you are angry.”

“It sang when we argued.”

“It sang when the children clapped yesterday too.”

“That was different.”

“Was it?”

Elara stood and went to the frame.

She put one hand near the glass without touching it.

“The glass tells us something happened strongly enough in the room to make it vibrate,” she said. “That is all we know from the glass.”

Hessa looked at Beren.

“I am angry, though.”

Beren looked surprised.

Hessa gave a short laugh. “You could have asked.”

The workroom settled for perhaps three minutes.

Then the real problem returned.

The tables did not fit the people.

One long table stood through the centre of the room. People carrying tools had to squeeze around it. The sign painters needed to spread paper flat, so their elbows reached into the walking route. The metal sorters kept asking others to repeat themselves because they were too far apart. Hessa, who worked with the nets, flinched whenever someone brushed unexpectedly behind her chair.

Everyone had a solution.

Move the tables together.

Move them apart.

Move them to the wall.

Use only two.

Bring in a fourth.

The glass sang so often that nobody laughed anymore.

Elara had an opinion.

She thought the central table should move against the window and leave a wide open route through the middle.

The answer felt obvious.

She nearly said it.

Then she remembered Beren pointing at the glass as if one sound had explained another person.

An obvious answer could still be only one person’s answer.

“What makes this arrangement hard for you?” Elara asked instead.

Nobody replied at first.

They were used to defending solutions, not describing problems.

Hessa went first.

“People pass behind me all day.”

Tavi lifted a hand. “If we move the metal table farther away, we cannot hear the part numbers.”

A sign painter said, “We need the daylight.”

Another person said, “I need somewhere I can work without three conversations beside my ear.”

Beren said, “I need people to stop treating loud as bad.”

The panel gave a faint hum when two answers overlapped.

Everyone looked at it.

Then, almost together, they looked away again.

They tried Elara’s preferred arrangement first.

The long table went against the window.

The middle of the room opened beautifully.

For half an hour, Elara felt pleased with herself.

Then the sign painters arrived.

“You took the light.”

Elara looked at the table.

They were right.

The painters’ paper had been pushed into the dimmer half of the room. One of them was holding two shades of yellow close to the window and frowning.

Elara felt heat rise in her face.

“I thought the route mattered more.”

“To you,” the painter said.

Not cruelly.

Just accurately.

Elara helped move the table back.

The glass hummed when one leg scraped across the floor.

“Apparently the table is furious,” Beren said.

Hessa snorted.

The second arrangement put all three tables in a row.

It gave the painters their light.

It gave Hessa a wall behind her chair.

It also put the metal sorters so far apart that they had to call numbers down the room.

“Seven-three?”

“What?”

“Seven-three!”

“Seven-eight?”

The glass began its thin song.

Tavi rubbed both hands over his face.

“This is worse.”

Elara sat on the floor beside the frame.

She closed her eyes for a moment.

Not to discover anyone’s feelings.

To separate the sounds.

Metal pieces clicking into trays.

Brushes in water.

Cloth shifting over the net table.

Tavi repeating numbers.

A stool scraping whenever someone needed the tool basket.

Footsteps crossing the same narrow strip.

The glass hummed when all of them piled together.

Elara opened her eyes.

“Could the work be different distances apart?” she asked.

Beren raised an eyebrow.

“That is a very Elara sentence.”

“I know.”

They tried again.

Two tables stayed close enough for tools and spoken numbers to pass easily between them.

The painting table moved toward the window but turned sideways, leaving a wider path beside it.

A small fourth surface—really an old door laid across two trestles—went into the quieter back corner. Nobody assigned it to a type of person. It was simply available when someone wanted more distance.

They moved Hessa’s chair so the main route passed in front of her rather than behind.

Then they worked.

For a while, the glass stayed quiet.

Elara noticed herself waiting for it.

She did not like that.

If the panel stayed silent, did that mean the arrangement was good?

No.

That would only be the same mistake in reverse.

She went to Hessa.

“How is the route?”

“Better.”

“To Tavi?”

“I can hear.”

“To the painters?”

One pointed to a long shadow crossing the paper.

“Fine now. Not fine after midday, I think.”

So after midday they shifted the painting table half a pace.

The glass never commented.

Later, Beren chose to work loudly.

He and Tavi were testing a set of small bells used to mark storage drawers. Each bell had to be struck, compared and labelled.

The room filled with bright metallic notes.

The glass sang hard enough to buzz in its frame.

Hessa looked up.

Beren froze.

“Do I have to stop?”

Elara looked at Hessa.

Hessa looked at the bells.

“I need warning before the next lot,” she said. “And I am going to the back table for ten minutes.”

Beren nodded.

“Fair.”

The bells continued.

The glass continued singing.

Nothing was wrong with either fact.

On the fourth day, a visitor entered while the panel was humming.

“Oh,” she said. “Someone upset?”

Beren opened his mouth.

Elara glanced at him.

Beren smiled.

“The room is vibrating.”

“That is all?”

“That is what the glass knows.”

The visitor looked disappointed.

Hessa laughed so suddenly that the glass answered with a clear bright note.

This time everyone laughed with it.

By the end of the week, nobody ignored the panel.

Its hum was useful.

Sometimes it reminded them that many sounds had piled up without anyone noticing.

Sometimes it led to a question about whether a route, distance or task needed changing.

Sometimes everyone was perfectly content and the room was simply loud.

The glass could not tell the difference.

People had to do that part with one another.

On the final afternoon, the rain repairs finished across the lane.

The three groups began carrying their baskets back to their usual rooms.

Elara helped lift the temporary trestle table.

When it was gone, the workroom suddenly felt enormous.

Beren stood in the open middle and clapped once.

The glass gave a small high answer.

“Still knows everything,” he said.

Elara picked up the last stool.

“Ask it how I feel about carrying this.”

Beren leaned toward the panel.

The glass reflected his face and said nothing.

He looked at Elara.

“Apparently I have to ask you.”

“Yes.”

“How do you feel about carrying that?”

“It is heavy.”

Beren took the other end.

Together they carried it out.
