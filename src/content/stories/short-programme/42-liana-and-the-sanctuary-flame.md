# Liana and the Sanctuary Flame

The old story called it a flame.

The cupboard held seeds.

Liana knew both versions.

Long ago, people had told a story about carrying a small sanctuary flame from one safe place to another so it would never go out. In the quiet room beside the shaded courtyard, nobody kept a magical fire.

They kept a wooden seed box.

Each packet had a name, a date and the place where the seeds had been gathered. Some held herbs. Some held climbing beans. Some held flowers that did well in shade. In spring, the gardeners chose what to plant in the courtyard beds and what to exchange with other villages.

The box mattered because living things did not always return by themselves.

Liana liked caring for it.

She liked the dry papery sound when packets slid into their compartments. She liked replacing a loose label before it fell off completely. She liked checking that the cupboard latch closed without crushing the corner of the lowest drawer.

Nobody had formally given her the job.

She simply noticed what needed doing.

At first, that was easy.

Someone returned a packet to the wrong compartment.

Liana corrected it.

A label peeled loose.

Liana pasted it back.

The cupboard grew damp after heavy rain.

Liana moved the box away from the wall and told the gardener.

Soon people began saying the same sentence.

“Ask Liana. Liana keeps the seed box.”

The first time, Liana felt pleased.

The fifth time, she did not notice.

By the twentieth time, the sentence had become part of the room.

One afternoon, Savi came in carrying three seed packets.

“Where do these go?”

Liana looked up from the book she was reading.

“Second drawer. Dry-bed plants.”

Savi went to the cupboard.

“Which part?”

“Left side.”

“There are four left-side compartments.”

Liana got up.

“This one.”

Savi put the packets away.

“Thank you.”

Liana returned to her book.

Three minutes later, an adult gardener appeared with a packet.

“Liana?”

She closed the book again.

That evening, she took the book home without having read the page.

Still, the seed box was safe.

That seemed more important.

Two days later, Liana went with her mother to the lower gardens.

She was gone for the whole afternoon.

When she returned the next morning, three packets were sitting on the quiet-room table.

They had been there since the previous day.

Liana stopped in the doorway.

Savi was folding cloths by the window.

“Why are those there?”

Savi looked at the packets.

“Oh. They came back yesterday.”

“Why did nobody put them away?”

“We did not know where.”

“You could have checked.”

Savi hesitated.

“We thought you would do it when you came back.”

Liana felt something tighten behind her ribs.

Not anger exactly.

Not only anger.

She had spent months making sure the box stayed orderly. Now three packets had been left out because she had been absent for one afternoon.

She picked them up too quickly.

One slipped.

Seeds rattled inside.

Savi reached for it.

“I have it.”

“I have it,” Liana said.

Her voice came out sharper than she intended.

Savi withdrew her hand.

Liana put all three packets on the cupboard shelf.

She knew where they went.

That was the problem.

Only she knew where they went.

She stood looking at the compartments.

The box had neat labels, but some labels used gardener abbreviations. Others referred to bed names that had changed. One drawer said SOUTH DRY even though the south bed had been moved two years ago.

Liana understood the system because she had watched it accumulate.

The system was not understandable merely because it made sense to Liana.

She left the packets on the shelf.

Savi looked surprised.

“Aren’t you putting them away?”

“Not yet.”

Liana found a blank card.

“What are you doing?”

“Making it possible for you to put them away.”

Savi came closer.

“Are you giving the job away?”

Liana looked at the seed box.

The question caught her.

She had not thought of the work as hers until someone suggested she might lose it.

“I am making it possible for somebody else to do the job,” she said.

“That sounds like yes.”

“Maybe some of it.”

They began with the drawers.

Liana wrote the names she used in her head.

Savi crossed out two immediately.

“Nobody calls that the south dry bed.”

“I do.”

“You are the only person.”

Liana stared at the card.

“Fine.”

They went to ask Maer Tessa, the adult gardener.

Tessa read the draft index.

“This one is wrong.”

Liana pointed. “No, the yellow-flower packets came from the ridge plot.”

“Some did. That packet came from the east wall bed.”

Liana checked the writing on the packet.

Tessa was right.

The place name had been shortened too broadly on the cupboard label.

Liana rubbed it out.

Savi watched.

“You do not know everything about the seed box.”

“No.”

“You looked like you thought you did.”

Liana gave her a look.

Savi smiled.

The new index took most of the morning.

They did not make it complicated.

Drawer one: food plants.

Drawer two: herbs.

Drawer three: flowers and ground cover.

Within each drawer, packets were grouped by the place and conditions written on the packet itself.

If a packet did not match the index, it went in a small CHECK tray rather than being guessed into a compartment.

Liana liked the CHECK tray best.

It meant not knowing did not have to become an error.

They practised with the three returned packets.

Savi placed the first two correctly.

The third did not fit.

“Check tray,” she said.

“Yes.”

“What happens to it there?”

“Someone who knows checks the packet.”

“Someone meaning you?”

Liana almost said yes.

“Tessa,” she said. “Or whoever is tending the beds that week.”

They added that to the card.

Over the next few days, several people used the cupboard without asking Liana.

At first, Liana noticed every time.

A man returned bean seeds.

Liana watched from the other side of the room.

He read the index.

Opened the correct drawer.

Paused.

Read the packet again.

Filed it.

Liana waited until he left.

Then she checked.

Correct.

The next person put a herb packet in the wrong compartment.

Liana felt vindicated for half a second.

Then she looked at the index.

The two labels were too similar.

She changed them.

The following day, Savi found a packet in the CHECK tray.

“I think this is ridge thyme.”

“Why?”

“The place says upper stones, and Tessa said that was the old name for the ridge plot.”

Liana looked at it.

“That sounds right.”

They asked Tessa anyway.

It was right.

Savi filed it.

By the end of the week, people had stopped saying, “Ask Liana.”

Mostly.

One person still did.

Liana pointed at the cupboard card.

“Try that first.”

He did.

It worked.

On the next market day, Liana left the quiet room before lunch.

She did not tell anyone she was leaving.

She went to the courtyard and helped two children build a line of flat stones around a new shaded bed.

At some point, she realised she had not thought about the cupboard for nearly an hour.

The thought made her uneasy.

She went back.

The seed box was closed.

Nothing was on the table.

She opened the cupboard.

A new packet sat in the flower drawer.

The label was complete.

Date.

Plant.

Collection place.

Someone had even written SHADE TOLERANT in the notes.

Liana checked the index.

Correct place.

She checked the packet.

Correct label.

She did not know who had filed it.

For a moment, she wanted to find out.

Then Savi appeared in the doorway carrying two cups.

“Everything all right?”

Liana looked at the packet again.

“Yes.”

“Did someone do it wrong?”

“No.”

Savi handed her a cup.

Liana closed the cupboard.

The latch clicked.

Outside, the courtyard was bright except beneath the long cloth shade where the new seed bed waited.

Liana and Savi went back out.

Later that spring, some of the stored seeds were planted.

Not all germinated.

The gardeners noted which ones failed and which grew.

The new packet became a row of small green leaves near the shaded wall.

Liana passed them one evening.

She did not know who had watered them that morning.

They had been watered.

She touched one leaf with the back of a finger and kept walking.
