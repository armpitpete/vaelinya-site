# Elara and the Storm of Voices

The storm had passed before breakfast.

The noise had not.

Rainwater still ran from the eaves of the south workhouse when Elara arrived with a basket of folded cloths. Inside, twelve people were speaking and almost no one was listening.

“The wool was here first.”

“The grain cannot stay wet.”

“We were told the west room.”

“The west roof leaks.”

“Then mend it.”

“With what time?”

A wooden stool scraped hard across the floor.

Elara stopped in the doorway.

The south workroom was usually a gentle place. Long tables stood beneath high windows. Hooks lined the walls. In autumn, bundles of herbs dried from the rafters and the room smelled of mint, dust and warm wood.

Today it smelled of wet wool, damp grain and people who had run out of easy patience.

The storm had broken tiles on two storage roofs.

One group had moved sacks of barley into the south room to keep them dry.

Another had carried in six baskets of washed wool before the rain reached the spinning shed.

Now both groups needed the same tables.

Talla, who was holding one end of a dripping wool cloth, pointed at a barley sack.

“That was put on our drying board.”

Benn, muddy to the knees, pointed at the rain outside.

“And the grain was meant to drown politely?”

“No.”

“Good.”

“That does not mean—”

Three people answered him at once.

The room filled again.

Elara put down her basket.

“Could we take turns?”

The nearest voices stopped.

For one hopeful breath, Elara thought the room had listened.

Then Talla said, “We have been taking turns being interrupted.”

Benn said, “And the barley does not get less wet while we practise manners.”

A woman by the far table laughed once without amusement.

Elara felt her face warm.

Taking turns had sounded sensible from the doorway.

Inside the problem, it was too small.

She moved farther into the room.

“Then not turns,” she said.

No one heard.

Elara climbed onto the low footstool beside the cloth basket.

She did not shout.

She picked up the wooden spoon that lay beside the kettle and struck the rim once.

Clonk.

The sound was plain enough to be surprising.

People looked.

“I do not know how to divide the room,” Elara said. “But I cannot understand what is needed while everyone is answering different questions.”

Talla folded her arms.

“What questions?”

Elara looked at the wet wool.

Then the grain sacks.

“What gets damaged first?”

That changed the room.

Benn looked down at the nearest sack.

“Barley if it stays warm and wet.”

Talla touched the wool cloth.

“This can wait folded for a little while. Not all day.”

A spinner near the window said, “The washed fleece at the bottom of those baskets will sour if we leave it packed.”

Someone else said, “The window tables have the best air.”

Another voice answered, “And the grain needs the floor space, not the tables.”

The disagreement remained.

But now the sentences touched the same problem.

Elara climbed down.

She took three pieces of chalk from the shelf and drew three headings on the dark slate board used for work counts.

NOW.

SOON.

CAN MOVE.

“Tell me if I put something in the wrong place,” she said.

Benn spoke first.

“Open the grain sacks now. They need air.”

Elara wrote it.

Talla said, “Spread the heaviest fleece now. The rest can stay covered for an hour.”

Elara wrote that too.

A man by the door said, “The grain sacks can move under the wall shelves if we lift them off the wet floor.”

Someone objected.

“Those shelves drip.”

“Only at the east end.”

“Then use the west half.”

Elara wrote the change.

The room became noisy again, but differently.

People still disagreed.

One spinner wanted all four window tables.

A grain keeper said two were enough.

Talla said three.

Benn said the question depended on how quickly the sacks could be turned.

Voices rose.

Elara lifted the spoon.

Then stopped.

The noise itself was not the problem.

Some of it was work.

She put the spoon down.

“Show it,” she said.

They stared at her.

“Show what you mean.”

Talla dragged one basket to the nearest table and opened the fleece.

Benn untied a barley sack and spread the mouth wide.

The spinner measured how much wool one table could hold without piling.

The grain keeper counted how many sacks could stand safely along the west wall.

The argument moved into hands, boards, widths and minutes.

Two tables for wool became three after the fleece was spread.

Three became two and a half when someone found a narrow drying frame behind the door.
Half a table sounded ridiculous until it was useful.

Benn still disliked the arrangement.

“This gives the wool the best air.”

Talla looked at him.

“Yes.”

“The grain was caught in the same storm.”

“Yes.”

Elara waited.

Talla touched the wet edge of the cloth.

Then she said, “If the barley starts warming, we move this basket first.”

Benn considered.

“That helps.”

He did not say it was fair.

Talla did not ask him to.

By midday, the south workroom looked untidy in a new way.

Barley sacks stood open on raised boards.

Fleece lay in broad pale sheets beneath the windows.

People crossed between them carrying bowls, cloths and dry wood.

The slate board had been wiped and rewritten three times.

NOW had become smaller.

SOON had become crowded.

CAN MOVE had become a list of things no one had expected to move.

Elara sat on the footstool with a cup of warm herb water.

Talla lowered herself beside her.

“You did not make us agree,” she said.

Elara looked across the room.

Benn was arguing with a grain keeper about whether one sack felt warmer than the others.

“No.”

“Good.”

Elara glanced at her.

Talla smiled.

“I was still angry.”

“I know.”

“No, you thought I needed quiet.”

Elara held the cup between both hands.

“At first.”

“I needed them to understand what the wool would do if it stayed packed.”

Elara nodded.

Across the room, Benn called, “Talla. This one is warming.”

Talla stood at once.

She checked the sack, then pointed to the first wool basket.

“That one can move. Give me a moment.”

The spinner beside her started lifting the fleece.

Outside, water still fell from the eaves in slow bright drops.

Inside, Benn objected to where the next sack was going. Talla answered him. Someone wiped NOW from the board and wrote it again smaller.

Elara sat on the footstool with her cup and listened while the room kept working.
