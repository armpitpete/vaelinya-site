# Valen and the Many Names

The old register had three spellings for what looked like one name.

Valen circled all three.

On the table beside him lay a bakery account, a roof-repair notebook and a copied list of winter rations. The papers came from different boxes in the archive and had been written years apart.

The names looked close enough to invite a decision.

Sarel.

Sarell.

Saren.

Valen drew a line between them.

Maer Olin, who looked after the archive room, stopped beside his chair.

“What proves those belong to one person?”

Valen looked at the line.

“The names are similar.”

“That proves they are similar.”

“The dates overlap.”

“That proves the dates overlap.”

Valen sighed.

Olin waited.

Nothing in the archive room hurried. Dust moved in the high window light. The shelves smelled of old cloth, paper and the cedar chips tucked between boxes.

Valen checked the bakery account again.

Sarel had delivered flour.

The roof notebook said Sarell had worked three mornings on the east storehouse.

The ration list gave Saren a household of four.

The same district appeared in all three.

That felt persuasive.

It was not proof.

Valen rubbed out the line.

The community history group was preparing a small display about the old market quarter. They had hoped the archive would make the work simple.

Instead every box added another version.

Nemi arrived carrying two family notebooks.

“My aunt said these might help.”

Valen opened the first.

Inside, a baker named Sarel appeared in a story about a collapsed oven wall.

“That is the bakery Sarel.”

“Maybe,” Nemi said.

“You sound like Nira.”

“I have been practising.”

The second notebook mentioned a roof worker called Ren Sarell.

Valen sat back.

“Ren.”

Olin looked over.

“What?”

“The roof notebook only used Sarell. This one gives another name.”

“So?”

“So maybe that is not the baker.”

Nemi leaned over the table.

“Or maybe the baker also repaired roofs.”

Valen frowned.

The new evidence had not solved the problem.

It had made the number of possible stories larger.

He disliked that.

A display needed labels. Visitors needed to understand what they were looking at. A table full of unresolved names felt like unfinished work.

Valen made three columns on a sheet.

**SUPPORTED**

**POSSIBLE**

**UNRESOLVED**

Under supported he wrote:

A person named Sarel appears in bakery records.

A person recorded as Sarell appears in roof-repair records.

A person recorded as Saren appears in the ration list.

Under possible:

Sarel and Sarell may refer to the same person.

Then he stopped.

“Why only those two?” Nemi asked.

“Because of the family notebook.”

“The family notebook makes Sarell less likely to be Sarel.”

“Right.”

“So write that too.”

Valen added:

Sarell may be Ren Sarell, named in a separate family notebook.

The clean story was disappearing.

That afternoon, the history group met in the old market hall.

They pinned copies of the records to a board.

A woman named Hessa read the labels.

“My grandfather used to call the baker Saren.”

Valen looked at her.

“Do you know whether that was the same person?”

“He said the baker had a bad left knee.”

“Do any records mention that?”

“No.”

Valen started writing.

Hessa raised a hand.

“I did not say it was true.”

“I know.”

“You are writing very quickly.”

Valen stopped.

“What would you like me to write?”

Hessa thought.

“That I remember my grandfather saying it. Not that the baker had a bad knee.”

Valen wrote exactly that.

The difference was small on paper.

It was not small in meaning.

Another visitor remembered that two cousins in the quarter had almost the same name.

A third said the roof worker had lived above a dye shop.

Someone else disagreed.

By the end of the meeting, Valen had more notes than when he started.

He also had less confidence in every simple connection.

Olin looked pleased.

“You enjoy this too much,” Valen told him.

“I enjoy records surviving contact with people.”

The next day Valen tried a different approach.

Instead of building one family tree or one biography, he made a source table.

Each row began with exactly what one source claimed.

Next came the date.

Then the place.

Then a final column:

**connection status**.

Some rows said **supported by another source**.

Some said **possible**.

Several said **unresolved**.

One said **contradicted by later account**.

Nemi read the table.

“It is uglier.”

“Yes.”

“I like it better.”

Valen looked offended.

“That was not the response I wanted.”

“It lets me see why you think things.”

That was better than neatness, even if he did not enjoy admitting it.

They tested the display with a small group.

A boy pointed at the three names.

“So which one is correct?”

“We do not know whether that is one name with different spellings or more than one person,” Valen said.

The boy frowned.

“Then why put it in the display?”

“Because the uncertainty is part of what the records tell us.”

“That sounds like not knowing.”

“It is.”

The boy accepted this much faster than Valen had.

Later, one of the adults suggested a title:

**THE KEEPER OF THE MARKET NAMES**

Valen stared at it.

“Who is that?”

“You, perhaps. You are organising the records.”

“No.”

The answer came so quickly that everyone looked at him.

Valen tried again.

“I am arranging this version of the display. That does not make the names mine.”

“Someone has to decide what goes on the wall.”

“Yes. For this wall.”

He pointed toward the archive room across the square.

“The records stay there. Other people can check them. Someone else can make a different display later.”

Olin nodded.

They changed the title.

**NAMES IN THE OLD MARKET RECORDS**

Less dramatic.

More accurate.

On the final afternoon, Hessa returned.

She brought a small folded note.

“My grandfather wrote this.”

Valen opened it carefully.

The note mentioned “Sarel at the ovens” and “Ren from the roofs” in the same sentence.

Valen felt a rush of satisfaction.

“There.”

Then he stopped.

The note strongly separated two of the names.

It did not explain Saren.

It did not prove every earlier assumption wrong.

It solved one part.

Valen updated the table.

Beside Sarel and Sarell he changed **possible same person** to **evidence supports different people** and cited Hessa’s note.

Beside Saren he left **unresolved**.

Hessa watched.

“You are not going to decide that one?”

“Not without more evidence.”

“Good.”

She took her note back after they copied it.

Valen did not ask to keep the original.

When the exhibition opened, visitors spent longer at the uncertain table than anyone expected.

Some traced the dates with their fingers.

Some offered memories.

Some simply read.

At closing time, Valen found a new paper tucked beside the display.

Someone had written:

**My mother used Saren as a nickname for Sarel. I do not know whether she meant this Sarel.**

Valen laughed.

Nemi looked over his shoulder.

“Problem?”

“More history.”

He added the note to the source list.

The display had begun with three names that looked as if they wanted to become one.

It ended with more names, more connections and more visible uncertainty.

Valen still liked clean accounts.

But he had learned not to make people clean for the sake of an account.

The archive could keep the records.

The community could keep adding to them.

No single person had to become keeper of the finished story.

Because the story was not finished.
