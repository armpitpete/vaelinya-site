# Liana and the Chair by the Wall

Every chair in the weaving room had a job.

That was the problem.

Six chairs stood at the long table for spinners.

Four low stools sat by the loom frames.

Two high-backed chairs faced the window for people mending fine thread in daylight.

Three benches ran along the warm wall for evening gatherings.

And one chair stood beside the door.

“Move that one in,” said Maer Tessa.

Liana had both hands on its back.

“Where?”

“Anywhere useful.”

Liana looked around.

The room was being prepared for the winter-pattern meeting. People would come after supper to choose which cloth designs the weaving house would make first: blankets, carrying wraps, curtain panels and heavy doorway cloth.

It would be busy.

The benches would fill.

The long table would fill.

Someone would bring tea.

Someone would speak too loudly over someone else.

Someone would say they did not mind where they sat and then mind very much.

Liana looked at the chair by the door.

“What if this is useful here?”

Maer Tessa followed her gaze.

“By the wall?”

“Yes.”

“For whom?”

“I do not know.”

“That makes it difficult to plan.”

Liana nodded.

It did.

She left the chair there.

Not in the doorway.

Not blocking the loom path.

Just outside the main circle, with enough room beside it for someone to pass.

Maer Tessa moved two baskets of yarn so the space stayed clear.

“You are responsible for explaining the mysterious chair,” she said.

Liana considered this.

“No.”

Maer Tessa looked at her.

Liana touched the chair back.

“If I explain who it is for, then I decide who should use it.”

“That is inconveniently sensible.”

“Yes.”

By evening, the weaving room was full.

People shook rain from their cloaks at the door. Boots squeaked on the floorboards. Cups clicked against saucers. Someone had brought honey cakes, which made the room smell warm and sweet beneath the wool, woodsmoke and damp cloth.

Liana sat on the end of a bench.

The chair by the wall remained empty.

Maer Tessa began the meeting.

“We have wool for three heavy runs before midwinter—”

The door opened.

A boy named Pell came in with his mother.

He stopped after two steps.

The room had become loud without anyone doing anything wrong.

Three conversations were ending at once.

A kettle lid rattled.

A toddler laughed from beneath the table.

Someone dragged a stool across the floor.

Pell’s mother leaned towards him.

“There is space on the bench.”

Pell shook his head.

She pointed at Liana’s end.

He shook his head again.

Liana did not wave him over.

She did not pat the bench.

She did not point to the chair.

Pell stood by the door for a moment.

Then he noticed it himself.

The chair by the wall.

He went to it.

Sat.

Put both feet flat on the floor.

His mother sat on the bench nearby.

The meeting continued.

Liana watched only long enough to see that nobody had blocked Pell in.

Then she looked back at Maer Tessa.

The first discussion was about blanket patterns.

People liked the old hill-stripe because it was quick to weave.

Someone wanted a wider border.

Someone else hated wide borders because they curled after washing.

The room grew louder.

Pell stayed in the chair.

After a while, he stood.

Liana noticed Maer Tessa notice him too.

Pell did not leave.

He stood behind the chair for several minutes.

Then sat on the floor beside it.

Nobody told him the chair was there for sitting.

Later, he moved to the bench beside his mother.

The chair became empty again.

That mattered.

It was not Pell’s chair.

It had not diagnosed him by waiting.

It was simply available.

Halfway through the meeting, an older woman came in carrying a basket of mending.

“There is nowhere left,” she said.

Maer Tessa pointed towards the wall.

“The chair is free.”

The woman sat in it immediately.

She did not need quiet.

She needed a chair.

Liana smiled.

A little later, the woman moved to the high-backed seat by the window when its owner left.

The chair by the wall became empty again.

Then a girl used it to hold a stack of pattern boards while she tied her boot.

Then nobody used it for almost half an hour.

At the end of the meeting, Maer Tessa gathered the selected patterns.

Pell had returned to the wall, but he did not sit.

He leaned against the stone beside the chair.

His mother asked, “Ready to go?”

“Not yet.”

“All right.”

She waited.

Liana began carrying cups to the wash basin.

A man stacking benches lifted the empty chair.

“Where does this live?”

“Here,” Liana said.

He looked around. “It is in the way of cleaning.”

“For cleaning, move it.”

He laughed. “Then where does it live after cleaning?”

“Here.”

He put it temporarily beside the loom.

They swept beneath it.

Then Liana returned it to the wall.

Pell watched.

“Is that chair for people who cannot sit with everyone?” he asked.

Liana shook her head.

“No.”

“For people who need quiet?”

“No.”

“For people who come late?”

“No.”

Pell frowned.

“Then what is it for?”

Liana looked at the chair.

“For being free.”

“That is not a proper job.”

“It was useful tonight.”

He thought about that.

“Yes.”

Behind them, two adults were still debating the width of the blanket border.

Maer Tessa came over carrying the last tea tray.

“I owe the mysterious chair an apology.”

Liana shook her head. “It does not mind.”

Maer Tessa looked at Pell. “Did it work for you?”

Pell shrugged.

“For a while.”

Then he added, “The floor was better later.”

Maer Tessa nodded. “Good to know.”

The next afternoon, the room was almost empty. A spinner used the chair to rest a sore ankle while winding yarn. After ten minutes she stood, pushed it neatly back toward the wall and returned to work. Nobody changed its name, added a sign or decided that this proved what the chair was for. Liana liked that best. A free place could be useful without becoming a category.

They finished cleaning.

The lights were lowered.

The benches returned to the wall.

The spinning chairs went back to the long table.

Every chair in the weaving room had a job again.

Except one.

One chair was allowed to remain unassigned.
