# Reo and the Upside-Down Tree

After the rain, Reo stopped beside a still pond.

A tree stood on the far bank.

Another tree seemed to hang upside down beneath it in the water.

Mara said, “It is only a reflection.”

“I know,” Reo said. “But it shows the bank differently.”

They were not meant to be studying trees at all.

Maer Siva had sent them to carry a bundle of dried reed ties from the garden store to the west shelter. Usually the route crossed the low path beside the pond. Two days of rain had spread the water across that path and into the grass beyond it.

A small board at the junction said:

**LOW PATH CLOSED WHILE FLOODED. USE HIGH ROUTE.**

The trouble was that neither Reo nor Mara could see where the high route began from where they stood.

“Back to the store and ask?” Mara said.

“That would work.”

Reo looked at the far bank again.

Reeds hid part of it. From their ordinary angle, the water seemed to meet the grass in one unbroken line.

The upside-down tree in the pond did not.

Its reflected branches were broken by a pale strip.

Reo moved one step sideways.

The strip vanished.

He moved back.

It appeared again.

Mara watched him.

“What are you doing?”

“Changing the problem.”

“You are walking sideways.”

“Yes.”

He moved farther along the dry edge. The reflection changed with him. The upside-down trunk stretched, narrowed and disappeared behind the reflected reeds.

At one angle, a pale band appeared between the dark water and the reflected branches.

Reo looked up at the real bank.

From the new position, the same pale band was visible through a gap in the reeds: gravel higher than the flooded grass.

“There.”

Mara squinted.

“That could be the high path.”

“Could be.”

“Or pale mud.”

“Also could be.”

They did not walk straight toward it.

The low grass between them and the far bank was still wet. Reo used the dry perimeter path to move around the pond until they could inspect the pale strip from closer ground.

It was gravel.

It curved behind a stand of willow and climbed gently toward the west shelter.

Reo grinned.

“Found it.”

Mara put one boot onto the gravel and pressed down.

The surface held.

Then she pointed ten paces ahead.

A section of the pale strip dipped beneath a sheet of shallow water.

“Found some of it.”

Reo's grin changed shape.

The reflection had helped them notice the path.

It had not inspected the path for them.

They walked as far as the dry gravel and stopped at the flooded dip.

A garden worker named Pell was clearing leaves from a drainage grate nearby.

“Is the high route open through there?” Mara asked.

Pell shook his head.

“Not all the way. The middle dip is still underwater. You can reach the shelter by taking the upper orchard edge.”

Reo looked behind the willow.

From here the orchard edge was obvious: a narrow line of compacted soil above the wet ground.

From the pond junction it had been hidden by reeds and the curve of the bank.

“So the high route changes routes,” Reo said.

Pell laughed. “The sign means stay off the low flooded path. It does not promise one perfect line.”

Reo liked that less than he liked a clever shortcut.

Which meant it was probably useful.

They followed the orchard edge.

At the next bend, Mara stopped.

“Which way?”

One track continued level toward the fruit store. Another climbed beside a stone wall.

Reo turned around.

From this direction, the shelter roof appeared above the hedge.

“Wall path.”

Mara checked the ground and nodded.

They reached the shelter without stepping into floodwater.

Siva was there stacking dry kindling.

“You found the high route.”

“Part of it,” Mara said. “Then another part.”

Reo put down the reed ties.

“The pond helped.”

Siva looked at the water through the trees.

“The pond?”

“The reflection.”

Mara added, “It made him walk sideways until he could see the gravel.”

Siva nodded. “That sounds more believable.”

On the return trip, Reo tried the same trick again.

He stopped beside a smaller puddle and looked for useful reflections.

All he found was a distorted piece of sky and Mara's face stretched sideways.

“Excellent,” Mara said. “What hidden route does my enormous forehead reveal?”

“None yet.”

“Maybe it is an oracle.”

“No.”

“Shame.”

At the pond, the wind had started to move the surface. The upside-down tree broke into shaking pieces.

Reo stood where he had first seen the pale strip.

Now he could barely see it in the water.

The real gravel path still existed.

“The reflection stopped helping,” Mara said.

“The path did not disappear.”

They walked around the dry edge again.

The next morning the water had fallen several hand-widths. Siva asked Reo and Mara to help update the route board with Pell.

Reo wanted to draw the high route as one clean line.

Pell stopped him.

“Yesterday the dip was closed. Today it is muddy but passable for people on foot. Tomorrow it may be dry.”

“So what do we draw?”

“Enough to help people check.”

They marked the low path as closed until the water fell below its stone marker. They marked the orchard edge as the current dry alternative. Beside the pond junction, they added a small arrow toward the place where the high gravel first became visible.

Reo looked at the finished board.

It was not a secret map revealed by water.

It was a current description made by people who had checked the ground.

That afternoon a visitor reached the board, looked at the arrow and asked, “So that way is definitely dry?”

Reo almost said yes. Then he saw Pell coming back from the orchard edge with mud on one boot.

“It's the current route,” Reo said. “Check the board and the ground. If conditions have changed, ask.”

The visitor nodded and took the marked path.

Mara, beside Reo, said, “Your map has become very unmagical.”

“Good maps usually are.”

Later, he stood by the pond one last time.

The tree on the far bank leaned over the water.

Its reflection leaned the other way.

Neither was more true.

One ordinary change of viewpoint had simply shown Reo something the first angle hid.

After that, the useful work was still looking up, walking closer, asking, testing and being willing to change route again.
