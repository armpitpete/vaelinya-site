# Liana and the Shaded Lantern

The trouble with the lantern was that everybody could see by it.

That sounded like a success until the evening work table filled.

Maer Yara had set the broad oil lantern in the middle of the room because six people were finishing different jobs before the morning market. Pell was sorting dark buttons into shallow trays. Savi was copying tiny stock marks from an old card. Mira was folding lengths of pale cloth. An older repairer named Tovan was threading needles. Liana had a basket of loose labels to tie onto finished bundles.

The lantern threw a clear circle across everything.

Savi leaned closer to her card.

“Can we move the light this way?”

Yara slid the lantern half a hand toward her.

Pell immediately turned his face aside.

“Too bright.”

Tovan looked up from his needle.

“Not bright enough here now.”

Yara moved the lantern back.

Savi sighed.

They tried raising it on a wooden block.

That spread the light farther but made the polished button tray flash whenever Pell looked down.

They lowered it again.

Tovan brought a second small lamp from the shelf.

For three minutes, that seemed clever.

Then the room was brighter everywhere.

Pell stood up.

“I am going to sort these tomorrow.”

“You do not have to stop,” Liana said.

Pell looked at the two lamps.

“I know. I am choosing to.”

Liana nodded.

That was different from being pushed out by the arrangement, but she still looked at the table after he left. His trays sat unfinished beside the empty chair.

Savi noticed too.

“I needed the light.”

“I know.”

Tovan held up his needle. “So did I.”

Nobody had done anything wrong.

The room was simply asking one lamp to be correct for several different bodies and several different jobs at once.

Liana went to the supply shelf.

There were scraps of thin wood, stiff cloth and two small frames used for drying painted cards. She picked up one frame and held a square of cloth against it.

“What are you making?” Yara asked.

“I do not know yet.”

“That is becoming a theme around you.”

Liana propped the cloth upright between the lantern and Pell's empty place.

The light on that side softened.

Savi looked down at her card.

“Mine is still bright.”

Tovan squinted at his needle.

“Mine is still bad.”

Liana moved the cloth panel a little.

Now Tovan's hands went dark.

“No,” he said.

She moved it back.

The panel helped one side but still behaved like a small wall. It had only traded one person's problem for another's.

Yara found three narrower pieces of stiff card.

“What if the shade belongs to the work place instead of the whole lamp?”

They set one small panel beside Pell's trays, angled so it blocked the direct glare but not the reflected light from the table. Another stood near Tovan's elbow to bounce more light from a pale card behind his hands. Savi kept the lantern side open.

They tested the places themselves before asking Pell to return.

Yara sat in his chair.

“Less glare.”

Liana sat there next.

“Still bright for me.”

“That is not very scientific,” Savi said.

“It is useful information about Liana,” Tovan replied.

Pell had not gone home. He was in the adjoining room helping stack cups. Yara asked whether he wanted to test the new arrangement.

“Test,” Pell said. “Not promise to stay?”

“Test.”

He came back and sat down.

Liana did not ask, “Better?” before he had even looked.

Pell sorted three buttons.

Then four.

He reached out and moved the little panel himself, turning it farther toward the lantern.

“That.”

Savi glanced over. “I can still see.”

Tovan threaded his needle.

“So can I.”

For ten minutes the table worked.

Then Mira changed jobs.

She finished folding pale cloth and began checking fine blue stitching near the hems. She leaned toward the lantern.

“I need more light now.”

Pell looked at her side of the table.

“You did not before.”

“I was folding cloth before.”

Mira pulled one unused panel toward her and angled a pale card behind the hem. The stitching became easier to see without changing Pell's side.

Pell smiled.

“That was not where you wanted it before.”

“It was right before,” Mira said.

Liana liked that answer.

The arrangement lasted another quarter hour.

Then someone opened the outer door and a cold draught made the lantern flame jump. Shadows moved across Savi's card.

She frowned.

“Now the panel makes it worse.”

She flattened hers.

Tovan shifted his chair because his back had begun to ache.

His good lighting angle disappeared.

He rebuilt it.

Nothing about the table stayed solved simply because it had once worked.

When Pell finished the first tray, he stood to stretch and left the shade where it was.

Liana said, “Do you want that place kept the same while you are up?”

He considered.

“No. If someone needs the space, they can move it. I can put it back.”

A younger girl used the chair for five minutes to count ribbon lengths. She pushed the panel away completely.

When Pell returned, he restored it without complaint.

By the end of the evening the table looked untidy in a useful way. Four little panels stood at different angles. One lay flat because nobody needed it. The lantern itself remained almost exactly where Yara had placed it at the start.

Savi finished her stock card.

Tovan threaded his last needle.

Mira tied the blue hems into a bundle.

Pell poured the final buttons into their marked jars.

Yara looked at the collection of improvised shades.

“So what is the correct arrangement?”

Pell pointed at his panel.

“This one for me, for buttons, tonight.”

Mira pointed at hers.

“This one for me, for stitching, until I was done.”

Tovan tapped the pale card by his elbow.

“And mine changed when I moved my chair.”

Liana picked up the panel nobody had used for half an hour.

“This one is correct in the cupboard now.”

Yara laughed.

They stored the pieces beside the lantern with a small note:

**MOVE THE SHADES. ASK THE PEOPLE. CHANGE THEM WHEN THE WORK CHANGES.**

The next evening the same table filled with different jobs.

The panels were arranged differently again.

Nobody treated yesterday's positions as instructions.

The lantern still guided the table.

Nobody needed the same amount of it in the same direction, and nobody had to prove that their preferred amount should become everybody else's.
