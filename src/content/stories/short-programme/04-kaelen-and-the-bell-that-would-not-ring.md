# Kaelen and the Bell That Would Not Ring

The noon bell moved.

It simply did not ring.

The rope came down.

The wooden arm swung.

The bell itself tilted against the pale sky.

Nothing.

People in the lower yard looked up anyway.

Someone laughed.

Someone else shouted, “Lunch must be cancelled.”

A woman carrying flour shouted back, “You may tell the ovens.”

Kaelen stood beneath the bell frame and watched it settle.

The bell was not large. It hung from a square timber frame beside the communal workshops, where its note marked the change between morning work and the midday meal. Children knew it. Bakers knew it. The dye house knew it. Even the goats seemed to know it, though Kaelen suspected the goats mostly knew when people started carrying food.

Rian pulled the rope again.

The bell swung.

Silence.

“That is unsettling,” he said.

Kaelen looked up at the frame.

“Do it once more.”

Rian pulled.

Kaelen did not watch the bell this time.

They watched the beam above it.

The left side dipped.

Only a little.

But enough.

“Stop,” Kaelen said.

Rian let go of the rope.

“You heard something?”

“No.”

“That is the problem.”

Kaelen pointed at the beam. “The frame moved.”

Rian squinted upward. “Frames move.”

“Not there.”

The workshop keeper came out carrying a mallet and three wedges.

“Clapper stuck?” she asked.

“Maybe,” said Rian.

“Frame first,” said Kaelen.

The keeper looked at them.

Kaelen pointed to the left upright.

A thin dark line ran down the timber just below the top joint.

The keeper’s face became serious.

“Everybody out from under it.”

That happened quickly.

The bell had been funny for exactly as long as nobody thought it could fall.

Now the yard cleared.

Kaelen liked that nobody argued with safety.

They set a rope boundary around the frame and fetched a ladder.

Rian reached for it.

Kaelen put one hand on the side rail.

“Not yet.”

“We have to see the crack.”

“Yes.”

“So I climb.”

“After we know the ladder is not leaning on the thing that is cracked.”

Rian looked at the frame.

Then at the ladder.

“Oh.”

The workshop keeper nodded. “Good catch.”

They brought two long support poles from the timber shed and set them against the sound side of the structure. Kaelen tested each foot against the paving stones. One rocked.

They moved it.

Tested again.

Stable.

Only then did the keeper climb.

Kaelen stood below, one hand on the support pole, feeling every movement pass through the wood.

The keeper examined the joint.

“The upright is split,” she said. “Old water got in around the peg.”

“Can we brace it?” Rian called.

“For now.”

“Then ring the bell?”

“No.”

Rian frowned. “If we brace it, why not?”

Kaelen answered before the keeper did.

“Because a brace can stop it getting worse without making it ready to carry moving load.”

Rian looked at them.

“You sound like Maer Delen.”

Kaelen considered this.

“Should I be worried?”

“Yes.”

The keeper laughed from the ladder.

They lowered the bell before doing anything else.

It took six people.

Not because Kaelen could not lift their share.

Because heavy things became safer when nobody needed to prove they could manage alone.

The bell settled onto folded sacks.

Up close, its silence became easier to understand.

The clapper moved freely.

The bell was not broken.

The problem was the structure that held it.

Rian tapped the bronze rim with one knuckle.

A soft clear note came out.

“There you are,” he said.

The bell had not lost its voice.

It had lost a safe place from which to use it.

They removed the cracked upright.

The damage ran deeper than it looked. Water had darkened the centre around the old peg-hole. A fresh split followed the grain almost halfway down.

“Replace it,” said the keeper.

Kaelen nodded.

Rian looked at the timber rack. “There is one the right length.”

He hurried towards it.

Kaelen followed more slowly.

The timber was the right length.

It was not the right width.

“Could trim it,” Rian said.

“Could.”

“Then let us.”

Kaelen ran a hand along the grain.

A knot sat near the place where the upper peg would need to go.

They shook their head.

“Not this one.”

Rian threw both hands out. “You say no very calmly.”

“It is still no.”

“We have spent half the meal break looking at wood.”

“The bell can wait.”

“Everyone else cannot.”

Kaelen looked across the yard.

People were already eating.

Someone had carried a red cloth around the workshops to mark the meal break instead. The ovens had sent a runner to the lower path. Children were passing the message from door to door.

The village had not stopped because the bell had.

“That part is working,” Kaelen said.

Rian followed their gaze.

His shoulders eased.

The bell mattered.

It was not the only way people could care for one another.

They found another upright behind the cart shed.

It was slightly longer than needed, with straight grain and no deep knots near the joint points.

This one they cut.

Kaelen measured twice.

Rian groaned theatrically during the second measurement.

Kaelen ignored him.

They cut the timber square, drilled the peg-hole, sealed the end grain, and fitted a small rain-cap above the joint so water would not sit there again.

None of it was dramatic.

Most of it was slow.

Shavings gathered around their boots.

Tools changed hands.

The keeper corrected the angle of the first brace.

Kaelen corrected their own measurement when they found the old frame was not perfectly square.

By late afternoon, the new upright stood in place.

The frame held its weight without the temporary supports.

Rian reached for the bell rope.

Kaelen looked at the keeper.

The keeper shook her head.

“Load test first.”

Rian looked betrayed by the whole idea of engineering.

They hung sacks from the bell arm.

One.

Two.

Three.

Kaelen watched the joint.

No dip.

They shifted the load sideways.

No twist.

The keeper checked each peg.

Then she nodded.

They removed the sacks and rehung the bell.

Rian handed Kaelen the rope.

“You found it.”

“You found half of it.”

“You stopped me climbing into the other half.”

“That too.”

“Ring it.”

Kaelen took the rope.

For a moment, everyone nearby looked over.

The yard became expectant.

Kaelen did not pull harder because people were watching.

They gave the rope one ordinary measured pull.

The bell swung.

Its note crossed the yard, round and warm.

People cheered.

Kaelen smiled.

Then the bell settled.

The timber frame stood quietly above it.
