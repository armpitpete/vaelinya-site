# Valen and the Pages Out of Order

The pages arrived in a flour sack.

This was not how the archive preferred to receive history.

Valen knew because Maer Sova looked into the sack, looked at the boy carrying it, then looked back into the sack as if a better explanation might have appeared between glances.

“What happened?” she asked.

“Roof leak,” said the boy.

“That does not explain the flour sack.”

“It was dry.”

Maer Sova closed her eyes briefly.

Valen leaned over the table.

Inside the sack were folded sheets, loose scraps, two pieces of thin bark-paper, a cover board with no pages attached to it, and something that might once have been a ribbon.

“Whose?” Valen asked.

“Old ferry records,” said the boy. “From the east landing store.”

Maer Sova opened her eyes.

That changed things.

The east ferry no longer ran. The river had shifted years ago, leaving the old landing beside a shallow reed bed. People still used the storehouse for nets, poles and weather boards, but the ferry records had been thought lost.

Valen reached into the sack.

Maer Sova caught their wrist.

“Dry hands.”

“They are dry.”

“Clean dry hands.”

Valen washed them.

Then they began.

The first page said:

*River high. North rope replaced. Three crossings delayed.*

The second said:

*White calf refused the ferry again.*

Valen smiled.

“Important history.”

“It was important to the person holding the calf,” said Maer Sova.

They laid the scraps across the long table.

Some had dates.

Some had page numbers.

Most had neither.

A few sentences began halfway through one sheet and seemed to continue on another.

Seemed.

That was where trouble started.

Valen found a fragment ending:

*...because the western post had moved.*

Another began:

*We tied the second rope before dark.*

They placed them together.

“There.”

Maer Sova looked over their shoulder.

“Why?”

“The post moved, so they tied another rope.”

“Could be.”

“That fits.”

“So does rain fitting a roof. It does not mean every wet floor came from the same hole.”

Valen frowned.

They moved the second page away again.

On another scrap they found:

*The children crossed first while the water was still low.*

That seemed to belong before a page describing a storm.

Then they found a later note:

*Children are not to cross first after what happened last spring.*

Valen sat back.

“Which spring?”

“No year?”

“No.”

Maer Sova shook her head.

They made three piles.

**Certain.**

Pages with dates, matching page numbers, or physical joins.

**Likely.**

Pages whose wording, ink or events seemed to connect.

**Unknown.**

Everything else.

Valen disliked the third pile almost immediately.

It was too large.

They moved one page from Unknown to Likely.

Maer Sova moved it back.

“You did not even read it.”

“I watched your face.”

“My face was reasoning.”

“Your face was tidying.”

Valen looked offended.

Then they looked at the table.

She was right.

They wanted the records to become a story.

Beginning.

Problem.

Storm.

Repair.

End.

But the pages had not promised them that shape.

A girl from the landing store arrived near midday carrying a wooden box.

“We found these under the shelf.”

More pages.

Valen almost cheered.

Then they opened the box.

The new pages made the old order worse.

One dated entry proved that a page Valen had placed near the beginning belonged two years later.

Another mentioned the western post being replaced, then repaired, then replaced again.

“How many times can one post move?” Valen asked.

“As many as a river asks,” said Maer Sova.

The girl pointed to a scrap near their elbow.

“That one is not a ferry record.”

Valen looked at it.

“Why not?”

“My grandmother wrote that hand. She kept the reed-cutting accounts.”

Valen compared the ink.

It looked similar to them.

“Are you sure?”

The girl nodded, then paused.

“I think so.”

Maer Sova smiled.

Valen moved the page to Unknown.

By afternoon, they had enough pieces to identify several clusters.

A flood year.

A winter repair season.

A period when the ferry carried building stone.

A later period when crossings became irregular as the river shallowed.

But between the clusters were gaps.

Valen stood at the end of the table and studied them.

“What if we arrange them by what probably happened?”

Maer Sova said, “Show me.”

So they did.

They placed the old ferry operating normally first.

Then the high water.

Then the damaged western post.

Then the second rope.

Then the changed crossing rule.

Then the shallowing river.

Then the final closure.

It made sense.

Too much sense.

The girl from the landing store pointed at the changed crossing rule.

“My grandfather said that rule came before the big flood.”

Valen looked at the dated flood page.

“Could he be remembering another flood?”

“Could you be joining the wrong pages?” she asked.

They opened their mouth.

Closed it.

“Yes.”

That answer made the arrangement feel less satisfying.

It also made it better.

Valen fetched small wooden labels.

They wrote:

**ORDER A — event sequence suggested by surviving pages.**

Then beneath it:

**Uncertain joins: 4. Missing intervals: at least 3.**

Maer Sova read the label.

“Good.”

Valen made another arrangement.

This one followed only dated pages, leaving wide spaces where nothing survived.

**ORDER B — dated sequence only.**

It looked ugly.

Honest things sometimes did.

Then the girl suggested grouping by subject instead of time.

Ropes.

Weather.

Animals.

Crossing rules.

Repairs.

Valen made **ORDER C**.

The white calf appeared twice.

That pleased them more than it should have.

At the end of the day, the table held three different useful shapes made from the same fragments.

None was complete.

None was nonsense.

A younger archive helper came in and stared at them.

“Which one is true?”

Valen looked at Order A.

It told the best story.

Order B showed what they could prove about time.

Order C made patterns of work easier to see.

They could have chosen one.

A part of them wanted to.

Instead they said, “They answer different questions.”

The helper frowned. “But what actually happened?”

Valen looked at the gaps between the pages.

“Some of it is here.”

They touched the dated records.

“Some of it is likely.”

They touched the inferred joins.

“Some of it is missing.”

They touched nothing.

The empty places mattered too.

Maer Sova brought three lengths of archive cord.

They tied each arrangement separately and placed the uncertainty labels on top.

The cover board went into the box beside them because nobody could prove which cluster it belonged to.

The flour sack was folded and returned to the landing-store boy.

“History survived a roof leak,” Valen said.

Maer Sova looked at the three bundles.

“Some history.”

Valen nodded.

The three bundles sat side by side on the long table, each with its own label. Between them, bare wood remained where no surviving page could tell them what came next.
