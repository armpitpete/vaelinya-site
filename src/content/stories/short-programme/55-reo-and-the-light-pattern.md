# Reo and the Light Pattern

The demonstration door had no handle on the visitors’ side.

That made everyone want to open it.

It stood at the end of the workshop hall in a wooden frame, with three sliding shutters mounted on a rail beside it. Above the shutters, a narrow roof window sent a strip of sunlight across the floor.

A painted sign said:

**TEST THE MECHANISM.  
DO NOT FORCE THE DOOR.**

Reo read the sign.

Then he tried the first shutter.

Nothing happened.

Second.

Nothing.

Third.

Nothing.

He slid all three open together.

Still nothing.

Tarin pushed gently at the door.

Reo pointed to the sign.

“That is not testing the mechanism.”

“I was testing the door.”

“The sign already told us that part.”

Tarin stepped back.

Maer Vessa, the adult running the workshop demonstration, stood on the far side of the barrier with her arms folded.

“Do you know how it works?” Reo asked.

“Yes.”

“Are you going to tell us?”

“No.”

“Good.”

Vessa smiled.

“Within the marked controls, you may test anything. I release the latch only after the correct sensor state holds. If you pull, wedge or climb, the test ends.”

A safety boundary.

Not a riddle with secret permission to ignore it.

Reo liked that.

He looked at the shutters.

Each had a different cut-out.

One square.

One narrow slit.

One circle.

The first idea arrived immediately.

“Shapes.”

He moved the square shutter until sunlight passed through it.

A bright patch appeared on the wall.

Nothing else happened.

Mira crouched beside the frame.

“There are pale marks here.”

Three small marks sat beside the door, faded enough to miss unless the light caught them.

Reo moved the square patch onto the first mark.

A tiny wooden indicator lifted.

Everyone shouted.

Reo grinned.

“One.”

He moved the slit onto the second mark.

The first indicator dropped.

“Not two separate switches,” Mira said.

“Or moving one changed the light on the first.”

Reo moved the square back.

The first indicator rose again.

He adjusted the slit without touching the square.

For a moment both indicators stayed up.

Then a cloud crossed the roof window.

Both fell.

Tarin groaned.

“The sky broke it.”

“No,” Reo said. “The light changed.”

“That is what I meant.”

They waited.

The sun returned.

Two indicators rose.

The third remained down.

Reo moved the circle toward the third mark.

The first indicator dropped again.

“Why?”

They all stared.

Mira pointed at the floor.

“When you moved the circle shutter, your arm blocked the square.”

Reo stepped aside.

The first indicator rose.

He laughed.

“We are part of the experiment.”

“Unfortunately,” Tarin said.

They tried the three marks again.

Square lit.

Slit lit.

Circle lit.

Nothing happened.

“Three should be better than two,” Tarin said.

“Why?”

“Because there are three.”

“That is not a reason.”

“It is nearly a reason.”

Reo moved the circle away.

The third mark went dark.

A faint click came from inside the frame.

Vessa lifted one finger.

“Hold.”

They froze.

After several seconds, a latch indicator turned.

Vessa opened the door from her side.

Tarin threw both hands up.

“Two light, one dark.”

Reo looked at the marks.

“Maybe.”

“Maybe? We opened it.”

“We produced one successful state.”

Vessa shut the door and reset the latch.

“Good distinction.”

Tarin frowned.

“You are both ruining success.”

Reo drew the shutter positions on a card.

Then he deliberately changed one thing.

He kept the first and second marks lit but moved the square patch partly off its mark.

No latch.

He restored it.

Latch.

He lit all three.

No latch.

He darkened the first and kept the second lit.

No latch.

He switched which physical shutter illuminated which mark.

That took longer.

The cut-outs changed the size and direction of each patch, so not every shutter could reach every mark cleanly.

Eventually Mira found a second arrangement that produced the same pattern: first and second marks lit, third dark.

The latch opened.

“That means the door does not care which shutter does it,” she said.

“It cares about the sensors.”

“Probably.”

Tarin sighed loudly.

“Can you say one sentence without ‘probably’?”

“Yes.”

He waited.

Reo did not give him another sentence.

They tested again.

A second cloud passed.

The light faded gradually.

This time the first indicator dropped before the second.

Reo marked that too.

“Different thresholds?”

Vessa did not answer.

The children moved a reflective board to strengthen the dimmer beam.

The first indicator returned.

The latch opened.

Now Reo had another question.

“Does the system require sunlight?”

Tarin pointed upward.

“Obviously.”

Vessa raised an eyebrow.

Tarin corrected himself.

“Apparently.”

At the side of the workshop sat a lamp used for close repairs.

They asked permission to move it.

Vessa checked that the cord would stay outside the walking path and agreed.

With the lamp, they reproduced the pattern.

The door opened.

“Not sunlight,” Mira said.

“Light above enough brightness at two sensors,” Reo replied.

“And darkness at the third.”

“Or light below its threshold.”

Tarin lay down dramatically on the floor.

“I have been defeated by precise language.”

Nobody helped him up.

Vessa finally opened the back panel of the demonstration frame.

Inside were three simple light sensors linked to wooden indicators and a timed release.

No magic.

No hidden judge.

No door that understood intention.

A mechanism.

Vessa pointed to the timer.

“The correct state has to remain stable for three seconds.”

“That was the delay,” Reo said.

She pointed to the third sensor.

“This one is a block condition. If it sees enough light, the release cannot engage.”

Mira looked at the shutters.

“So we were not drawing a picture.”

“No.”

Tarin sat up.

“I said shapes.”

“So did I,” Reo said.

“And we were wrong?”

“We were partly useful.”

Tarin considered this.

“I accept partly useful.”

Before leaving, Reo made a card for the next visitors.

Mira read it and stopped him.

“You wrote the answer.”

The card said:

**LIGHT MARKS ONE AND TWO. KEEP THREE DARK.**

Reo looked at it.

“That ruins the test.”

He turned the card over.

On the other side he wrote:

**CHANGE ONE THING AT A TIME.  
WRITE DOWN STATES THAT FAIL AS WELL AS STATES THAT WORK.  
DO NOT FORCE THE DOOR.**

Vessa nodded.

“That can stay.”

The next group arrived.

A girl moved all three shutters immediately.

Nothing happened.

Reo nearly told her where the pale marks were.

He stopped.

She found them herself.

Then she made a pattern Reo had never tried.

It failed.

She wrote it down.

Tarin whispered, “Should we help?”

“She is testing.”

After a while the girl discovered the two-light pattern.

The latch clicked.

She cheered.

Reo smiled.

Her route to the answer had not been his route.

The rule was the same.

That was the useful part.

Later, walking home, Tarin said, “It still felt like a puzzle door.”

“It was a puzzle.”

“Then what was different?”

Reo thought about the moving maze, the echo puzzle, the spiral stair and all the other problems where a clever route or changed assumption mattered.

“This one did not reward cleverness,” he said. “The mechanism did the same thing every time. We had to learn what condition it responded to.”

“And if we had guessed correctly first?”

“We would still need to test whether the guess was actually the rule.”

Tarin groaned.

“You would test breakfast.”

“If breakfast had three sensors and a latch.”

The workshop door closed behind them.

Its pale marks disappeared into shadow.

Nothing about it had chosen Reo.

Nothing about it had wanted to be understood.

It had simply behaved.

And because it behaved consistently enough to be wrong about, Reo could learn something real from it.
