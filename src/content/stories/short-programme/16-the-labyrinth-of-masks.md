# The Labyrinth of Masks

The costume hall had been rearranged for the midsummer procession.

Usually it was one long room with storage racks along the walls and a clear path from the street door to the courtyard.

For procession week, painted screens divided it into changing spaces, repair bays, prop stores and narrow passages. Costumes hung from rails in bright layers: fox tails, moon-grey cloaks, silver ribbons, red drummer sashes, painted wooden masks.

Signs hung everywhere.

**FOXES THIS WAY.**

**MOON DANCERS LEFT.**

**DRUMMERS THROUGH BLUE SCREEN.**

**UNMASKED PERFORMERS — COURTYARD DOOR.**

The arrangement worked beautifully on the steward’s plan.

Then people arrived.

Some performers changed roles after rehearsal began.

Some wore parts of several costumes.

Some kept masks on while moving between rooms.

One child had no role at all; they were carrying water and spare pins.

Another performer simply did not want to announce which group they belonged to every time they needed the toilet, the courtyard or the repair table.

By midday, people were walking in circles.

A fox dancer emerged from behind the blue screen carrying a drum sash and said, “I have been past this table three times.”

The hall steward, Maer Sen, looked at the children.

“I need help before full rehearsal.”

Fina was already tying on a plain practice mask.

“I will run the route.”

“Which route?” Reo asked.

“The procession route.”

“Which procession role?”

Fina stopped.

“That is the problem, isn’t it?”

She pulled the mask down over her face. It had no colour, symbol or role mark.

“I am nobody the signs know.”

She entered the first passage at normal rehearsal speed.

At the first junction she found:

**FOXES RIGHT. MOON DANCERS LEFT.**

No courtyard.

No repair table.

No exit.

Fina chose right.

The passage narrowed between two screens and ended beside a rack of fox tails.

She returned.

“Failed.”

Reo grinned.

“That sounds like a sign problem pretending to be a person problem.”

Nira ignored the signs and walked the room slowly.

She noted what did not depend on a costume.

Three tall north windows.

A stone washbasin near the centre wall.

A red strip painted across the floor where an old partition had once stood.

The wide courtyard doorway.

Two fixed pillars.

The street door.

“These can identify places without identifying people,” she said.

Valen read the nearest sign.

“It tells a story about who a person is before it tells them where they are.”

He turned it over.

On the blank side he wrote:

**COURTYARD — FOLLOW RED FLOOR STRIP TO WIDE DOOR.**

Fina tried again.

This time she reached the courtyard.

“Fixed.”

“No,” Lina said from the entrance.

Fina groaned.

“What now?”

“I asked three people what is making the hall difficult.”

Lina had spoken separately with performers waiting outside.

One wanted to keep a full mask on until the procession began.

Another needed a private place to change a costume layer.

A third was happy to name their role when it helped but did not want role disclosure to become the price of finding the exit.

A fourth used a mobility frame and could not turn through one of the narrow passages at all.

Lina had not brought names back with the accounts.

“Why no names?” Valen asked.

“They did not need to be part of the route problem.”

Sen nodded.

They tested the wide-route issue first.

Kaelen took an empty costume rack through the hall because it was roughly as wide as the mobility frame.

At the red strip, the rack caught a screen foot.

He stopped.

Fina said, “Angle it.”

Kaelen did not.

“If a route works only by forcing equipment around a bad corner, it is not a wide route.”

With Sen, he shifted one temporary screen and stabilised its feet farther back.

The turn opened.

That changed another passage.

A small changing bay now faced directly into through-traffic.

Lina looked at Sen.

“That breaks the privacy request.”

They moved a second screen.

That created a pocket of space near the central junction.

Liana sat there for a minute.

People streamed past on three sides.

“No.”

“No what?” Reo asked.

“If this becomes the waiting place, people will be surrounded by movement.”

Sen pointed to an unused alcove beside the washbasin.

“What about there?”

Liana tried it.

The alcove was outside the main route and visible from the courtyard path without being in it.

“Better.”

“Quiet waiting?” Valen asked.

“Waiting,” Liana said. “Do not decide why someone uses it.”

He wrote:

**WAITING AREA — KEEP PASSAGE CLEAR.**

Not **QUIET PEOPLE**.

Not **ANXIOUS PERFORMERS**.

Not **MASKED WAITING**.

Just a place.

They replaced several role-first signs with destination signs.

**COURTYARD.**

**REPAIR TABLE.**

**PRIVATE CHANGING BAYS.**

**PROP STORE.**

**STREET EXIT.**

For an hour the hall seemed solved.

Then full rehearsal began.

Two large groups entered at the same time.

The new signs worked perfectly.

Everyone knew where to go.

They all went there together.

The passage at the courtyard door jammed.

A drummer carrying a frame drum stopped behind a moon dancer with a wide shoulder piece. Someone trying to reach the repair table came from the opposite direction. Fina, testing the route at procession pace, reached the back of the crowd and nearly walked into it.

“Stop.”

Elara’s voice was calm, but everyone heard it.

The hall settled.

“The directions are clearer,” she said. “The timing is not.”

Reo looked at the two approaches.

“Separate routes?”

“Maybe,” Elara said. “But do not change the route before we know whether timing alone fixes it.”

They ran the rehearsal again with staggered departures.

Fox performers first.

Then moon dancers.

Then drummers.

It worked until a performer left the moon group halfway through to get a broken clasp repaired.

They entered the repair route against the next flow.

Collision again.

Fina said, “Then nobody changes route during movement.”

Lina shook her head.

“The repair table exists because people need to change route.”

Elara looked at the junction.

“Then the route needs a way to admit interruption.”

Liana pointed to the waiting alcove.

“One group can hold there without blocking the passage.”

Sen added a small reversible sign at the junction:

**COURTYARD FLOW ACTIVE — WAIT HERE IF CROSSING.**

It was not tied to a role.

Anyone could use it.

The next run worked better.

Then Nira noticed people pausing at a sign beside the north windows.

It said:

**RED STRIP TO COURTYARD.**

A stack of folded cloth had been placed over part of the strip.

“The landmark disappears when storage changes.”

Valen looked annoyed.

“It was a good sign.”

“It was accurate this morning.”

They did not delete the red strip reference.

They added the fixed north windows and washbasin as backup landmarks.

**COURTYARD — TOWARD NORTH WINDOWS, PAST WASHBASIN, THROUGH WIDE DOOR. RED STRIP MAY HELP.**

Reo read it.

“That is a paragraph.”

“Improve it.”

He did.

They tested a symbol map: window shape, basin shape, wide-door shape.

Some people understood it immediately.

Others preferred words.

So they kept both.

No one routing method became compulsory.

The next complication came from the masks themselves.

A performer in a silver bird mask stopped at the repair sign.

**MASK REPAIR — ASK INSIDE.**

Valen reached for it.

“We are replacing role labels.”

Lina stopped him.

“Keep that one.”

“Why?”

“Because it describes what someone wants to do, not who they are.”

The performer nodded through the mask.

“I need a strap replaced. The sign is useful.”

Valen left it.

That distinction changed how they reviewed the rest of the hall.

A sign that said **DRUM STORAGE** could stay.

A sign that said **DRUMMERS ONLY** needed a reason.

A sign that said **PRIVATE CHANGING — OCCUPIED/AVAILABLE** could stay because it described the space.

A sign that said **UNMASK HERE** came down immediately.

Sen looked embarrassed when he removed it.

“It was meant to help people organise before the courtyard.”

“It makes removing a mask sound like the price of continuing,” Lina said.

Sen nodded.

They replaced it with:

**COURTYARD AHEAD. COSTUME CHECK AREA OPTIONAL.**

During the next rehearsal, the performer in the silver bird mask kept the mask on through the whole hall.

Nobody stopped them.

Another performer removed theirs in the private bay.

Nobody treated that as more authentic.

A third wore no mask at all.

The route did not care.

Late in the afternoon, Sen asked the children to run a deliberately difficult test.

He changed three things without telling them which.

One screen moved.

One storage rack blocked a familiar landmark.

One departure order changed.

Fina entered in the plain mask again.

At the first junction, she found the route narrowed.

Kaelen stopped her.

“Do not squeeze through. We test width again.”

The empty rack failed the turn.

Sen moved the screen back.

At the second junction, the red strip was hidden.

Nira used the windows and washbasin.

At the third, two groups arrived together.

Elara held one flow.

Liana opened the waiting alcove.

Reo checked whether the side route could take the smaller group.

It could.

Lina asked the actual performers whether anyone needed the private bay before the flow resumed.

One did.

Valen updated the temporary route board so the change was visible rather than held in one person’s memory.

Fina began the next movement only after those conditions were ready.

They reached the courtyard.

Sen looked at them.

“Which one of you solved it?”

Reo pointed at the ceiling.

“The building.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is as good as asking which one of us solved it.”

Fina pulled off the plain mask.

“I could test speed and movement, but I could not tell which landmarks survived.”

Nira said, “I could identify stable places, but not what people needed to keep private.”

Lina said, “I could bring those needs in, but not make a screen stand safely.”

Kaelen tapped the nearest screen foot.

“I could make the physical route stable, but not stop two good routes colliding in time.”

Elara said, “I could separate flows, but not decide where someone should be sent.”

Valen held up the route board.

“I could make the current arrangement visible, but I needed Reo to keep finding alternatives when it failed.”

Reo bowed.

Liana pointed to the alcove.

“And alternatives need somewhere to wait without becoming another blockage.”

Sen nodded.

The next morning the full procession dressed in the hall.

The room was still complicated.

Costumes remained complicated.

People remained more complicated than costumes.

One performer changed from fox to drummer after a costume tore.

The signs still worked.

One child waited in the alcove without explaining why.

Nobody asked.

A masked performer used the wide route to the courtyard.

Nobody asked them to show their face.

A person carrying water ignored all role signs because the directions no longer depended on role.

At one point the courtyard flow became too crowded and Elara held the next group for half a minute.

Fina did not treat waiting as failure.

When the passage cleared, she moved them on.

Then an adult visitor entered from the street carrying a box of spare costume pins. She was not a performer, did not know any procession roles and had never seen the hall before.

“Repair table?” she asked.

She followed the destination sign, used the north windows when a costume rack hid the red strip, waited while a group crossed, and reached the table without telling anyone who she was or what group she belonged to.

Nira watched her go.

“That is a better route test than us pretending to be confused.”

Valen added **NEW USER — NO ROLE KNOWLEDGE — ROUTE SUCCESSFUL** to the temporary board.

The system had survived someone who did not know the assumptions it was designed to remove.

Valen stood near the old stack of discarded signs.

**FOXES THIS WAY.**

**MOON DANCERS LEFT.**

**UNMASKED PERFORMERS.**

They were not evil signs.

They had simply assumed that knowing a person’s category was the easiest way to tell them where to go.

That assumption had turned a room into a labyrinth as soon as people stopped fitting neatly.

The new hall still had masks.

It still had roles.

It still had private spaces and group spaces and signs that named activities when naming the activity actually helped.

What it no longer demanded was an identity before offering a direction.

And nobody had to remove a mask to prove they deserved a way through.
