# Kaelen and the Standing Stone

On exhibition day, almost everything in the courtyard moved.

Tables moved because the wind changed.

Paintings moved because sunlight reached them.

A basket display moved because one of its legs sank into soft ground.

People moved because all the other things had moved first.

The standing stone did not.

It rose near the north wall, dark and broad enough that three children could sit against its warm side after midday. It was older than the exhibition, older than the courtyard paving and, according to Pela, probably older than Maer Doven's jokes.

Doven did not deny this.

Because the stone was easy to find from almost anywhere in the courtyard, the exhibition team placed the current task board beside it.

At each bell, helpers were supposed to return there, read the latest board, and collect a new task slip if their work had changed.

Kaelen liked the arrangement immediately.

“This stays here,” he said.

“The stone does,” Doven replied.

“The board too?”

“Today, yes.”

“And the task slips?”

Doven smiled. “Those are exactly the things most likely to change.”

Kaelen's first slip said:

**HELP AT BLUE CLOTH TABLE UNTIL SECOND BELL. THEN CHECK BOARD.**

He folded it carefully and put it in his pocket.

The blue cloth table held pottery pieces from three workshops. Kaelen's job was to keep the pieces inside their marked spaces while visitors looked at them and to tell Maer Savi if a stand became unstable.

The first hour went well.

A small bowl nearly rolled when a child bumped the table. Kaelen steadied the display board without touching the bowl itself and called Savi. She added a low wooden stop at the edge.

At the second bell, Pela appeared.

“Stone.”

“I know.”

They returned together.

The board had changed.

Blue cloth table remained open.

The print table had moved under the east awning.

The woven screens had swapped positions with the basket display.

Kaelen's new slip said:

**MOVE TO EAST AWNING. HELP KEEP PRINT QUEUE CLEAR. THIRD BELL: CHECK BOARD.**

He read it twice.

“East awning,” Pela said.

“I can read.”

“I am celebrating your achievement.”

Kaelen ignored her.

At the east awning, the job was different. People waiting to try the print blocks kept drifting into the narrow space where finished sheets were hung to dry. Kaelen and an adult helper moved a cord line half a pace outward and asked visitors to wait behind it.

By the third bell, Kaelen felt as though he understood exhibition day.

Things changed.

The stone stayed.

You returned to the stone.

You read the new task.

Simple.

Then the bell rang while Kaelen was helping an older visitor find the basket display.

The visitor walked slowly and wanted to stop twice to look at other things.

By the time Kaelen reached the standing stone, several helpers had already taken their new slips.

His said:

**RETURN TO BLUE CLOTH TABLE. HELP CLOSE DISPLAY FOR WIND.**

Kaelen put it in his pocket and went.

At the blue table, Savi was not closing the display.

She was reopening it.

The gust had passed.

“Good,” Kaelen said. “I can help reset it.”

Savi looked at him.

“What task do you have?”

“Blue table.”

“Which board time?”

Kaelen took out his slip.

Third bell.

Savi pointed toward the standing stone.

“Fourth-bell update went up early. Wind plan changed.”

Kaelen frowned.

“The fourth bell has not rung.”

“No. Doven rang the small hand bell at the board because the weather changed quickly.”

Kaelen had not heard it from the east side.

His slip was real.

It had been correct when he received it.

It was not current now.

Pela arrived carrying a stack of empty card frames.

“You are supposed to be at the quiet table,” she said.

Kaelen looked at her frames, then at his slip.

“No, I am supposed to be here.”

Pela held up her own slip.

**KAELEN — QUIET TABLE, WEST WALL. HELP REPLACE DIRECTION CARDS.**

“That has my name on it.”

“Yes.”

Kaelen did not like the feeling that followed.

The old note in his hand felt solid. The new information had arrived somewhere else. For a moment he wanted Savi and Pela to agree that his slip should remain right because he had followed it properly.

Instead he said, “I need to check.”

They returned to the standing stone.

The board now showed a large heading:

**CURRENT AFTER WIND CHANGE**

Under it, the blue cloth table had reopened with adult staff only. Kaelen's name appeared beside the west-wall quiet table.

Doven stood nearby answering questions from two other helpers.

Kaelen held out his old slip.

“This says blue table.”

“It did,” Doven said.

“When did it stop being the current instruction?”

“After the gust inspection. We changed the task board and sent hand-bell notice around the courtyard. You were probably between stations.”

“I did not hear it.”

“Then you could not act on it.”

Kaelen looked at the new board.

“So I am not in trouble?”

“No.”

“Then why does this feel like I did something wrong?”

Doven considered.

“Because you followed an old instruction carefully and still ended up in the wrong place.”

Kaelen nodded.

“That is a terrible system.”

“It would be if the old slip were expected to update itself.”

Pela tapped the stone.

“This part does not update either.”

“No,” Doven said. “The stone only gives us a place we can all find again.”

Kaelen folded the old slip differently and put it in a separate pocket from the new one.

At the west-wall table, he helped replace three direction cards that still pointed toward displays moved earlier in the day. One card said **PRINTS → NORTH** even though the print table had been under the east awning for hours.

A visitor read it and started north.

Kaelen called, “That card is old. Current print table is east.”

Then he took the card down.

The mistake felt familiar.

Information did not become bad because it had once been correct. It became dangerous when nobody could tell whether it was still current.

Before the fifth bell, Kaelen returned to the standing stone early.

Pela joined him.

“You are becoming enthusiastic about checking.”

“I am becoming suspicious of pockets.”

The board had changed again.

This time his task stayed the same.

Kaelen read it anyway.

Near closing time, a younger helper named Jori stood beside the stone looking close to tears.

“My table is gone,” he said.

“Which table?” Kaelen asked.

“The red ribbon table. I was told to bring these there.”

He held a tray of small clay tokens.

Kaelen could have told him where he thought the table had moved. He had seen something red near the south wall ten minutes ago.

Instead he said, “Let's check the current board.”

They found **RED RIBBON DISPLAY — LOWER HALL DUE WIND**.

Jori exhaled.

“Do you know where the lower hall is?” Kaelen asked.

Jori nodded.

“Want help carrying the tray?”

“Yes.”

They went together.

By closing time, the courtyard looked completely different from the morning. The blue table had folded away. The print station was indoors. Half the direction cards had been removed. The standing stone cast a long shadow across empty paving.

Kaelen returned the final task slip to Doven.

“The stone was useful,” he said.

Doven nodded.

“It never told me what to do.”

“No.”

“It did not make the information permanent.”

“No.”

“It just stayed somewhere we could go back to.”

“That is sometimes enough stability to make change manageable.”

Kaelen leaned one hand against the stone.

Tomorrow the exhibition would be gone.

The board would be removed.

The stone would still be there.

That did not mean tomorrow's answers were already written beside it.
