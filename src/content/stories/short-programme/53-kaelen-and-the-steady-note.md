# Kaelen and the Steady Note

The rehearsal kept losing its beginning.

Not the first beginning.

That one was easy.

Maer Iven raised a hand, counted four beats, and the first group entered together.

The trouble came when the second group joined.

Then the third.

By the time the round was fully moving, one line rushed, another dragged, and someone inevitably stopped because they could no longer tell which beat belonged to them.

They tried again.

And again.

The rehearsal room grew warmer.

A window fogged at the corners.

Someone dropped a folded song sheet and did not bother picking it up.

“This is getting worse,” Mira said.

“It is getting louder,” Teren replied.

“That too.”

Kaelen stood near the back wall.

He was not there to make the music beautiful.

Elara was not there either.

Nobody needed someone with special access to the song.

They had a practical problem.

The singers needed a reference stable enough to find their entrances while everything else moved around it.

Kaelen tapped four slow beats against his knee.

“What if one note stays underneath the first two rounds?”

Iven looked at him.

“A drone?”

“Only for the entries.”

Teren tried a low note.

It wobbled because he was laughing.

“Not you,” Mira said.

“You do it.”

She tried.

Her note climbed whenever the melody above it climbed.

Kaelen understood the difficulty.

Holding something steady while everyone else changed was not the same as being unaffected by them.

“I can try.”

He chose a low note that sat below the round.

Plain.

Unimportant.

The first group entered.

Kaelen held the note.

The second group found its place.

Then the third.

For the first time that afternoon, the round survived all three entrances.

People smiled while they sang.

Kaelen felt the note vibrating in his chest.

It was almost too satisfying.

When the round ended, Iven said, “Again.”

They repeated it.

Better.

Again.

Better still.

By the fourth attempt, one singer had begun watching Kaelen rather than listening to the group.

On the fifth, two did.

Kaelen stopped after the first entry.

The round collapsed.

“What happened?” Mira asked.

“I stopped.”

“We noticed.”

“I think you were using me as the count.”

Teren shrugged.

“You were the count.”

“No. The note was one reference.”

“What is the difference?”

Kaelen thought.

“If I am the count, then when I stop, the whole thing has no count.”

Mira looked at the others.

“That appears to be what happened.”

Nobody argued.

Kaelen disliked how much the result proved his concern.

They tried again with Teren holding the low note.

Teren managed one round.

Then his pitch slipped.

Mira laughed.

He stopped and bowed.

“Stable as a falling bucket.”

They tried a quiet tap on the bench instead.

That helped the timing but was harder for the far side of the room to hear.

Iven moved the bench.

The tap became easier to hear, but now the person tapping could not see the third group’s entrance.

“Good,” Kaelen said.

“Good?” Teren asked.

“We are finding out what each reference is bad at.”

Mira pointed at him.

“That sounded like Nira.”

Kaelen ignored her.

They made a small list on the board.

LOW NOTE — easy to hear, hard to hold.

BENCH TAP — easy to keep steady, not easy to hear everywhere.

HAND SIGNAL — easy to see, impossible if you are looking at the page.

COUNTED BREATH — useful only at the start.

Nobody had one perfect answer.

They rearranged the singers into a wider curve.

The bench moved to the middle.

Mira tapped.

The first round held.

Then the bench leg scraped against the floor and the tap vanished under the noise.

The second group missed its entrance.

Someone groaned.

Kaelen laughed.

“What now?” Iven asked.

“Try two references.”

Mira tapped.

Teren held the low note.

The singers started again.

This time, if someone lost one cue, the other remained.

The round held for three full cycles.

Then Teren ran out of breath.

He stopped.

The singers continued for another cycle using only the tap.

Mira stopped tapping.

The singers continued again.

They had learned the shape well enough to carry it themselves.

The room went strangely still after the final note.

One singer said, “Kaelen’s note fixed it.”

Kaelen shook his head.

“It helped us find the problem.”

“And fixed it.”

“No.”

He pointed to the board.

“We used four different things.”

“Your idea started it.”

“That is different.”

The singer considered.

“Fine. Your idea started us fixing it.”

Kaelen accepted that.

The next day, the group rehearsed again without him.

Kaelen arrived late carrying a basket of repair pegs for Iven.

He stopped outside the door.

The round was already moving.

No low note.

No bench tap.

No hand signal.

The singers had changed where they stood. The second group had moved closer to the first. The third had marked their entry directly on the page. One person quietly counted with a finger against their own sleeve.

Different people were using different supports.

The round held.

Kaelen entered only after they finished.

Teren saw him.

“You missed your job.”

“What job?”

“Being indispensable.”

Kaelen smiled.

“Good.”

Iven handed him the empty peg basket.

“We did keep one thing.”

Kaelen looked around.

“What?”

“The list.”

The board still showed all four references and their limitations.

Beside them someone had added:

**REMOVE WHEN NO LONGER NEEDED.**

Kaelen read it twice.

“That is not a musical instruction.”

“No,” Iven said. “It is better.”

A younger singer at the end of the curve still struggled with the third entrance.

“Can I have the tap back?” she asked.

Nobody told her the group had progressed beyond it.

Mira pulled the bench closer and tapped quietly for her part alone.

After two rounds the singer raised a hand.

“Stop now.”

Mira stopped.

The next entrance landed cleanly.

Kaelen noticed that too.

Removing a support did not prove strength. Keeping one did not prove failure. The useful question was whether the person using it could say what helped and whether the arrangement could change when the answer changed.

They began another round.

This time Kaelen sat near the wall and listened.

One entrance came a little early.

The next corrected.

Nobody stopped.

Nothing held the whole room together.

Several people held enough of it, for long enough, and handed the work on.

The song did not need a root that could never move.

It needed supports people could share, test and let go.
