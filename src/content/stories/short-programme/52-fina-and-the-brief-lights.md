# Fina and the Brief Lights

The first bright streak crossed the evening sky before the observation sheets were ready.

Fina saw it from the orchard path, a white line so quick that by the time she pointed, there was only dark blue sky and a thin bank of cloud above the western ridge.

“They have started.”

Bram looked up from the bundle of boards under his arm.

“You are sure?”

“I saw one.”

Maer Dalen, who had organised the night watch, checked the little sand timer hanging from her belt.

“That is about when the shower was expected,” she said. “It may last an hour. It may be shorter if the cloud comes in.”

That was enough for Fina.

“Then everyone should go to the ridge platform now.”

She was already turning toward it when Dalen said, “Everyone?”

Fina stopped.

The ridge platform had the widest view, but it was also the farthest marked place from the hall. Three observation stations had been prepared: the ridge platform, the east court and the orchard wall. Each had a board, a charcoal stick and a sheet ruled into narrow time boxes.

Fina had helped make them.

She had still imagined everyone using the best one.

Another streak flashed high in the north.

Bram missed it because he was looking at Fina.

“That is two,” she said.

“And we have recorded none,” he replied.

The urgency in his voice matched the urgency in hers. Fina liked that. It made moving fast feel obviously right.

She lifted two boards.

“Ridge.”

Sella, who was carrying the third, looked toward the east court.

“There is a clear patch there.”

“The ridge is clearer.”

“For now.”

Fina followed Sella’s glance.

A long grey cloud was sliding over the ridge from the west. The east court had less sky overall, but its open patch faced north-east and was still clean.

The orchard wall showed only a strip between two roofs, but Bram was already standing beside it.

“Keep all three,” Fina said.

The decision felt slower than running to the ridge, even though it took only a few seconds.

Dalen nodded.

“Good. Nobody has to chase the same patch of sky.”

Fina carried the first board uphill.

The platform boards were cold under her hands. Below, the orchard lamps had begun to glow. Someone in the courtyard shut a door, and the sound carried clearly in the evening air.

Fina set the observation sheet down.

Another streak appeared.

She marked the time box and drew a short line.

Then another.

Then nothing.

For several minutes the sky stayed empty.

Fina shifted from one foot to the other.

From the east court, Sella called, “One!”

Fina looked across the roofs but saw only cloud.

A moment later Bram called from the orchard wall.

“Two close together!”

Fina gripped the charcoal.

The best place was producing the worst record.

She looked west.

The cloud had spread over half the ridge view.

“We should move,” she said.

Dalen, who had followed more slowly, came up beside her.

“Where?”

“The east court.”

“Why?”

“Because Sella can still see them.”

“Can you see the whole east court sky from here?”

“No.”

“Then what do you know?”

Fina hated questions whose answer was less useful than the thing she wanted to do.

“I know this station is getting worse.”

“That is enough to change this station.”

Not all stations.

Fina understood the correction before Dalen said it.

She picked up her board.

Halfway down the path, a bright streak cut across the one remaining clear gap behind her.

Fina turned too late.

“Did you get it?” she shouted.

Dalen had stayed on the platform.

“Yes.”

Fina laughed despite herself.

At the east court, Sella was kneeling on the paving stones with her sheet held flat by two pebbles.

“I thought you were staying on the ridge.”

“It stopped being useful.”

Sella pointed upward.

“Here is still useful, but only there.”

The clear patch was smaller than Fina had expected. A roof edge cut off the low sky. The old bell tower blocked part of the east. But the north-east remained dark and open.

They watched.

A streak flashed.

Sella marked it.

Fina saw a second one lower down.

They both reached for the charcoal.

“Yours,” Sella said.

Fina wrote the time.

For ten minutes they worked well together.

Then two more people arrived from the hall.

“Is this the best place?” one asked.

Fina nearly said yes.

Instead she said, “It is clear in this direction right now. The orchard wall has another view.”

One of them stayed. The other went to Bram.

The distinction mattered.

A useful place was not automatically the place everyone should use.

The shower grew brighter.

Streaks came in uneven bursts: nothing for three minutes, then two almost together, then a long pause.

Fina began guessing when the next burst would come.

After one quiet stretch she said, “Now.”

Nothing happened.

Sella smiled.

“You told the sky.”

“I made a prediction.”

“A very commanding prediction.”

Fina smiled too.

A streak appeared thirty seconds later.

“That one does not count as me being right.”

“Good.”

They kept watching.

Then a shout came from the ridge.

Not Dalen.

Bram.

Fina looked around in surprise.

He had moved from the orchard wall without telling them.

“Brightest one yet!”

Fina saw only its fading end.

For one hard second she was furious.

She had missed it.

She had organised the stations. She had moved when the ridge clouded. She had kept watching. And the brightest streak had appeared somewhere else.

Then she realised Bram was laughing.

He held up his board.

“I got it!”

Fina's disappointment did not disappear, but it changed shape.

The event had not been lost.

Only she had missed it.

When the cloud finally covered the whole northern sky, Dalen called the watch.

They carried the boards into the hall.

The room smelled of warm wood, lamp oil and the soup someone had left near the stove. Everyone crowded around the long table.

Fina laid the three observation sheets side by side.

They did not match.

The ridge sheet had strong records at the beginning and one bright streak near the end.

The east-court sheet had the longest continuous run in the middle.

The orchard sheet had fewer marks overall but included the brightest streak and two low ones hidden from the other stations.

Bram tapped his page.

“If we had all gone to the ridge, we would have missed these.”

Sella tapped hers.

“If we had all stayed here, we would have missed his.”

Fina looked at the sheets.

Her first idea had not been foolish. The event really had been brief. Waiting too long would have lost observations.

But she had treated urgency as if it required one movement, one pace and one place.

“What did the three stations do better than one?” Dalen asked.

“Different views,” said Sella.

“Less moving,” said Bram.

Fina thought about it.

“And nobody had to be everywhere.”

Dalen nodded.

That was the part Fina wanted to remember.

The next morning she helped copy the observations into the shared record. Beside each mark they wrote the station that had seen it. Where two stations had recorded what was probably the same streak, they marked the match as probable rather than certain.

Fina was tempted to draw one grand total at the bottom.

She stopped.

“Do we know whether some of these are duplicates?”

“Not all of them,” Bram said.

“So the total would pretend.”

They left the total blank.

That evening, when Fina passed the orchard wall again, the sky was ordinary.

No shower.

No white lines.

Nothing waiting for her to organise it.

She stood for a moment where Bram had stood.

From there, the strip of sky really was narrow.

It had still mattered.

Fina smiled and went inside.

The best record had not come from finding one perfect place.

It had come from letting several useful places remain useful at the same time.
