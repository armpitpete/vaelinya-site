# Reo and the Bridge with Three Bends

The bridge was straight.

That was the problem.

Reo stood on the bank and looked at it.

Six long planks crossed the irrigation channel from one stone landing to the other. Two side rails ran along them. A small handcart waited behind Reo with three sacks of turnips inside.

On the far bank, the path continued towards the kitchen gardens.

Between Reo and the far bank, one middle support had sunk.

The bridge dipped sharply in the centre.

“Do not,” said Mara.

Reo turned.

“I have not done anything.”

“You are looking at it sideways.”

“I am looking from here.”

“You know what I mean.”

Mara was eleven and had been told to bring the turnips to the kitchen store before the afternoon rain. She liked routes that worked because they had already worked yesterday.

Today’s route had stopped cooperating.

A repair worker crouched at the near landing, measuring the sunken support with a pole.

“No cart across,” he said.

Mara folded her arms. “How long?”

“Until we reset the centre stone.”

“How long is that?”

“Longer than the turnips want.”

Reo looked upstream.

There was another bridge, but reaching it meant climbing to the upper terrace, crossing, then coming back down. The turnips would arrive late. Nobody would starve. But Mara had promised the kitchen.

“Could we carry the sacks one at a time?” she asked.

The worker shook his head. “Not on that deck. The middle planks are twisting because the support is low.”

Reo crouched.

The bridge was not broken everywhere.

That seemed important.

The first two planks were firm.

The next pair dipped.

The final two rose again to the far landing.

The rails were still solid because their posts stood outside the sunken centre.

Reo walked along the bank.

“What if the bridge did not go straight?”

Mara closed her eyes.

The worker looked interested.

Reo pointed at the near rail post, then at a flat maintenance stone halfway along the channel edge, then at the far rail post.

“Here. Then there. Then there.”

“That is not a bridge,” Mara said.

“It is three small bridges pretending not to know each other.”

The worker stood.

“Show me without moving anything.”

Reo used a stick to draw in the mud.

The first section would run diagonally from the near landing to the maintenance stone beside the sunken centre.

The second would cross the narrowest part of the channel at an angle.

The third would reach the far landing.

A crooked route.

Mara stared at the drawing.

“The cart cannot turn like that.”

Reo looked at the cart.

She was right.

He had solved the bridge and forgotten the wheels.

“That version is bad,” he said.

Mara blinked.

“You gave up quickly.”

“I found the part that fails quickly.”

He drew again.

“What if the cart stays here?”

“We need the turnips there.”

“Turnips do not have wheels.”

Mara looked at the sacks.

The worker rubbed his chin.

“Hand-carry?”

“Maybe.”

“Still needs a safe crossing.”

“Yes.”

Reo liked maybe better when adults said it.

They did not take planks from the damaged bridge. That would have made the repair harder and might have shifted the load.

Instead the worker fetched three short maintenance boards from the tool shed and two rope handlines.

Reo helped lay the first board.

It looked good.

The worker pressed down on it with one boot.

The maintenance stone rocked.

“Bad,” Reo said.

“Yes.”

They lifted the board away.

Under the flat stone, water had washed out the soil.

Reo stared at the hole.

Mara said, “So now the crooked bridge is broken too.”

“It was never a bridge yet.”

That distinction mattered.

An idea was not safe because someone could imagine it clearly.

They moved the first landing point farther upstream to a larger stone set into the bank.

The angle became sharper.

Mara tried walking it on dry ground before any board crossed water.

“Too tight with a sack,” she said.

Reo carried an empty sack and tried.

His shoulder caught the rope handline.

He stopped.

“Too tight.”

They widened the first turn.

The second board now needed to be longer.

The worker found one.

They tested again.

This time Reo could carry the empty sack through all three bends without touching the rails.

Mara tried.

At the second bend she froze.

The water below was shallow, but fast.

“I do not like this.”

Reo looked at her feet.

She had stopped before the narrow board.

“We can use the upper bridge.”

Mara frowned. “After all this?”

“Yes.”

“You do not mind?”

“I mind the hill more than I mind changing my mind.”

The worker said, “We can also test with weight before you decide.”

They filled an old grain sack with stones equal to one turnip sack.

The worker carried it across first.

The boards flexed but held.

He came back.

Then they tied the weighted sack to a rope and dragged it through the bends without a person on the boards.

The first section held.

The second held.

At the third bend, the sack swung outward and struck the rail post.

Reo stared.

“If that was Mara’s shoulder—”

“It would hurt,” said the worker.

“And could turn her.”

“Yes.”

Mara looked pale.

Reo moved the third board.

Not much.

Just enough that the last bend opened wider.

They dragged the sack again.

No strike.

Again.

No strike.

The worker checked every board, rope and landing point.

Then he crossed carrying half a sack of turnips.

Then Mara crossed empty-handed.

Slowly.

At the far bank she turned around.

“That was all right.”

“Do you want to carry one?” Reo asked.

Mara considered the boards.

“One small one.”

They divided the turnips into lighter loads.

It took longer than Reo’s first idea.

It took longer than Mara wanted.

But it took less time than the upper bridge route, and nobody had to trust an untested shortcut.

Reo carried the final sack.

At the second bend he stopped and looked down at the damaged straight bridge beside him.

From here, he could see the sunken support clearly.

The old bridge had been built for carts, many people, and an easy straight crossing.

Their temporary route was worse at all of those things.

It was also good enough for one specific job under one specific condition.

That difference pleased him.

On the far bank, Mara loaded the sacks onto a garden barrow.

“You know this is not a better bridge,” she said.

Reo grinned.

“I know.”

“It is slower.”

“Yes.”

“It cannot take carts.”

“Yes.”

“It has three ridiculous bends.”

“Yes.”

Mara looked back at the temporary boards.

“But it got the turnips across.”

Reo nodded.

The worker had already begun taking the first board up again.

“Temporary route,” he said. “Then proper repair.”

Reo helped him coil the handline.

The three boards came up one by one, leaving the damaged straight bridge exactly where it was, waiting for its proper repair.
