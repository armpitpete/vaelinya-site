# Kaelen and the Winter Watch

Kaelen liked watches because they had edges.

Begin here.

End there.

Pay attention in between.

The winter watch at the south hall seemed especially clear. Snow was falling steadily over the courtyard path. From a warm window seat, one person watched three dark route markers outside. When snow covered the lowest stripe on a marker, they told the hall steward, who sent an adult team to clear and remark the path.

Kaelen's turn was one hour.

He had a slate, a cup of warm water and Maer Doven in the room behind him.

Before the watch began, Doven showed him the rota.

Sera would take the second hour.

Benn would take the third.

If the weather worsened, adults outside would decide whether the courtyard route stayed open at all. Kaelen's task was observation and reporting, not deciding that people should cross because he thought he could still see the stones.

“I know,” Kaelen said.

Doven tapped the rota.

“And your hour ends here even if the snow does not.”

Kaelen looked at the falling white beyond the window.

“That seems badly coordinated.”

“The snow was not consulted.”

At first the work was easy.

Marker one blurred.

Kaelen noted the time.

Marker two stayed clear.

A carrying group crossed the courtyard.

Marker three collected snow on its north side.

Kaelen noted that too.

He liked the rhythm of it. Look. Record. Look again.

Doven occasionally asked what had changed since the last check. Kaelen answered without having to search the slate.

Halfway through the hour, the outside clearing team passed marker one and brushed snow from its dark stripes. Kaelen recorded the reset time.

“Good watch,” Doven said.

Kaelen sat straighter.

Ten minutes later he realised he had read the same line on his slate twice.

He did not mention it because he had corrected himself.

Then he forgot whether he had already drunk from the warm cup.

That did not seem important.

When Sera arrived to take the next watch, Kaelen looked outside.

“I can keep going.”

Sera took off her gloves. “You can also finish.”

“I am not tired.”

He was not, exactly.

He simply did not want to leave while the snow was still falling.

Sera had come from the flour house and looked tired enough already.

“I will do another half hour,” Kaelen said. “Then you start.”

Sera looked at Doven.

Doven looked at Kaelen.

“Whose decision is the handover?” he asked.

Kaelen knew the answer.

“Both people's.”

Sera sat at the other end of the bench.

“Half an hour,” she said. “I watch too.”

For the first ten minutes, Kaelen was pleased with the arrangement.

Then he wrote marker two when he meant marker three.

He noticed and corrected it.

Five minutes later, Doven asked whether marker one had crossed its clearing line.

Kaelen looked up quickly.

It had.

He had not seen when.

Sera had.

“About two minutes ago,” she said.

Kaelen stared at the slate.

He was still sitting upright. He could still see. He could still answer questions.

His attention had become less reliable before he felt dramatically tired.

“That is annoying,” he said.

Sera smiled. “Yes.”

Kaelen handed her the slate.

The handover took less than a minute.

He showed her the corrected entry, the last clear time for marker one and the cup that needed refilling.

Then he stood.

“Where are you going?” Doven asked.

“Food.”

“Excellent winter strategy.”

Kaelen ate in the adjoining room and discovered that he had been hungry enough to finish before noticing the taste.

He stayed there longer than he intended. From the bench near the stove he could still hear people moving in the watch room, but he no longer had to decide what every sound meant.

When he returned, Sera was watching the courtyard.

She pointed at the spare chair.

“You may sit without being responsible.”

Kaelen sat.

For a while, he watched Sera watch.

She noticed different things. Kaelen had tracked the depth marks carefully. Sera noticed that wind was beginning to fill one side of the path faster than the other.

She asked Doven to have the outside team check marker three earlier than the ordinary interval.

They did.

Snow had drifted around its base even though the visible stripe still looked clear from the window.

Kaelen frowned.

“I did not notice the drift direction.”

“You were watching the stripe,” Sera said.

“That was the task.”

“It was one part of the task.”

Kaelen looked at her slate. She had added a small note beside the marker numbers: **wind increasing from west**.

His own entries were not wrong. They were incomplete in a way another watcher had noticed.

When Sera's hour ended, she handed the slate to Benn.

“You are finished?” Kaelen asked.

“Yes.”

“Even though the snow is still falling?”

“That is why Benn is here.”

Benn waved from the window seat.

Kaelen looked at the three of them, then at the slate passing from hand to hand.

The watch had not weakened when he left it.

It had continued.

After supper, the snow became heavier.

Doven asked whether Kaelen wanted another shift later in the evening.

Kaelen's first answer rose automatically.

Yes.

Then he checked himself before speaking.

His eyes felt gritty. He had read the same page of a small book three times without taking it in. The thought of sitting at the window felt satisfying because the job was clear, not because he was ready to do it well.

“Not tonight,” he said.

Doven nodded and wrote someone else's name on the rota.

Kaelen felt a brief, sharp disappointment.

Then relief arrived behind it.

The next morning he took an early watch after sleeping.

The courtyard looked transformed. Snowbanks edged the cleared path. Two of yesterday's route markers had been moved by the adult team because the safest line had shifted away from a drift.

Kaelen read the overnight notes before starting.

Sera's wind observation had become important. Benn had recorded when the west edge began filling fastest. Another watcher had noted that one marker was becoming difficult to see from the window even before snow reached its lowest stripe.

Kaelen added those facts to his attention rather than deciding his old method had been useless.

At the end of his hour, the next watcher arrived.

Kaelen gave the slate over on time.

“You can keep going if you want,” the watcher joked.

Kaelen looked outside.

The snow was still falling.

“No,” he said. “That is why you are here.”

Outside, the courtyard needed steady watching for many hours.

Inside, nobody had to become steady for all of them.
