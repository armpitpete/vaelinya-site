# Fina and the Echo Step

The west stair answered every foot differently.

Fina had known this since she was small enough to climb it on both hands.

The stair rose beside the old seed house in twelve broad stone steps. On one side stood a low wall warm with moss. On the other, the ground dropped into a narrow yard where empty drying frames leaned against the building.

If you walked up normally, the steps made ordinary sounds.

Tap. Tap. Tap.

If you ran, the wall threw the sound back.

Tap-tap. Tap-tap.

If you jumped from the seventh step to the ninth, which adults had explained was unnecessary, the seed-house door gave a flat wooden thump in reply.

Fina liked the west stair.

Today she liked it less.

“Again,” said Maer Olin.

Fina stood at the bottom with a coil of red cord over one shoulder.

At the top, Olin held the other end.

Between them, the cord crossed three chalk marks on the stair.

The old carrying rail had been taken down for repair. Until it returned, the seed keepers needed a safe way to move baskets up and down without people meeting in the narrowest part and knocking each other sideways.

Fina had offered to test a temporary route.

Her first route had failed.

Not badly.

Badly enough.

She had run up the left side while Olin came down the right with an empty basket. They reached the seventh step together. Fina turned her shoulders. Olin turned his basket.

The basket struck the wall.

The wall answered with a hard clap.

Fina’s loose waist tie caught the basket handle.

For one complicated moment, they became one person with too many elbows.

Olin sat down.

Fina stayed standing only because the red cord pulled tight across her knees.

Neither fell into the yard.

That counted as useful information.

“Too narrow at seven,” Fina said.

Olin rubbed his elbow. “That is one way of describing it.”

“I can fix it.”

“You can test a different way.”

“That is what I meant.”

Fina moved the chalk mark from step seven to step five.

“Up here,” she said. “People change sides earlier.”

Olin looked at the stair.

“Why?”

“More room.”

“There is more room on step five?”

Fina looked.

There was not.

Step five only felt wider because the moss wall dipped there.

She crouched.

“Fine. Not five.”

From the seed-house yard came the dry rattle of bean pods being poured into trays. Someone laughed. A wooden scoop knocked against a basket.

Fina stared at the stair.

She wanted to move.

Thinking was easier when her feet had something to do.

She ran up.

The wall returned her steps in quick little bursts.

Tap-tap.

Tap-tap.

Tap-tap.

At the seventh step, the echo thickened.

Tap-tap-THUM.

Fina stopped.

She ran back down.

At seven, again:

Tap-tap-THUM.

She climbed slowly.

Tap.

Tap.

Tap.

On the seventh step she stamped once.

THUM.

Olin leaned over the top rail post.

“Have you started arguing with the stair?”

“It started.”

Fina stamped the sixth.

Tap.

The seventh.

THUM.

The eighth.

Tap.

She frowned and pressed her palm to the wall beside step seven.

Cold stone.

Then she pressed the top of the step with both hands.

The slab shifted so little she might have imagined it.

Fina pressed again.

A grain of grit jumped at the back edge.

“Olin.”

He came down carefully.

Fina stepped aside.

He put his weight on the seventh stone and rocked once.

The slab clicked under his heel.

“Loose,” he said.

Fina felt a little heat rise behind her ears.

She had been planning traffic around the narrow place.

The narrow place was not the whole problem.

“Would it have moved if we had not bumped?” she asked.

“Probably.”

“Would we have noticed?”

“Eventually.”

Fina looked at the drop beside the stair.

Eventually was not a good word near a drop.

Olin fetched a chalk stub and drew a thick cross over the seventh step.

“Closed until it is reset.”

Fina watched him.

Then she looked at the remaining eleven steps.

“We still need a route.”

“We do.”

“We can use six and eight?”

“We can test whether we can.”

That was different from yes.

Fina liked it.

They fetched two empty baskets and a sack filled with old cloth.

Fina carried the sack first.

Instead of deciding the whole route before moving, she tested one action at a time.

She climbed to six.

Stopped.

Turned sideways.

Olin came down to eight and stopped above the crossed step.

They could see each other clearly.

There was space for the baskets.

Fina stepped back down.

“Again, but you move first.”

They did.

This time Olin descended to eight. Fina waited at four, then climbed to six.

No collision.

Again.

Fina moved first.

Again.

They carried wider baskets.

Again.

They tried with Fina talking the whole time, because people on seed-house errands rarely travelled in ceremonial silence.

That failed in a different way.

Olin could not hear her “hold” over the bean trays rattling in the yard.

Fina listened to the west stair.

Tap.

Tap.

Tap.

The wall gave each step back.

She smiled.

“What if the stair tells us?”

Olin folded his arms.

“The stair has already told us quite a lot.”

Fina took the red cord and tied one loose wooden ring to the wall post beside step six and another beside step eight.

She knocked the lower ring once.

Tok.

The wall returned it.

Tok.

Olin knocked the upper ring twice.

Tok-tok.

The wall returned two smaller knocks.

Fina tried from the bottom.

One knock: someone waiting below.

Two knocks: someone waiting above.

Three quick knocks: stop.

Olin added the important rule.

“Silence does not mean safe. It means no signal.”

Fina nodded.

They tested it until the signals stopped feeling clever and started feeling ordinary.

Then they asked other people to try.

Sera from the drying yard knocked too softly. The wall swallowed the sound.

They changed the rings for thicker pieces of ash wood.

Tovin knocked three times when he meant two because his basket shifted.

They moved the upper ring so a person could reach it without changing grip.

A small child named Pela forgot the pattern entirely.

Fina opened her mouth to explain it again.

Pela said, “Why not paint one mark here and two marks there?”

Fina closed her mouth.

Then she laughed.

“Because that is better.”

By late afternoon, the stair had two painted signal marks, two wooden knockers, a chalk cross over the loose seventh slab, and a very simple rule:

See the mark. Knock. Wait for the answer. Move only when both people know who is holding.

The seed keepers tried it with real baskets.

No elbows joined together.

No handles caught Fina’s waist tie.

No one stepped on the loose stone.

At the bottom, Olin gave her the red cord to coil.

“You began with a route,” he said.

“I ended with a conversation.”

“With a stair.”

“With everyone using the stair.”

Olin nodded.

Fina wound the cord around her hand.

The west wall caught the late light. Moss shone along its top edge. Someone climbed from the yard and knocked once before the narrow section.

Tok.

A moment later, two knocks came from above.

Tok-tok.

The walker waited.

Then moved.

Fina felt the answer in her feet before she heard it.

She had not guessed the stair correctly.

She had tried it, listened to what happened, and changed the next step.

That was slower than being right at once.

It was also much more useful.
