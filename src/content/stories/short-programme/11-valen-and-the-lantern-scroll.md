# Valen and the Lantern Scroll

The scroll said three different things depending on where Valen put the lamp.

That was already a reason not to trust the first one.

Maer Sova had found the rolled sheet in a storage chest beside old maintenance records. In daylight, most of it looked blank. A few dark lines survived near the edges, but the centre had faded almost completely.

“Old route map?” Valen asked.

“Maybe.”

“Repair list?”

“Maybe.”

“You are enjoying this.”

“A little.”

They unrolled it beneath two smooth weights.

When Valen placed a lantern directly beside the sheet, faint brown writing appeared where the light passed through thinner fibres.

They read aloud:

“Lower wall. Three posts. West channel.”

Sova wrote those words on a fresh scrap.

Then Valen moved the lantern to the other side.

The words changed.

Not magically.

Different marks became visible.

Shallow pressure lines crossed beneath the faded ink. Someone had once written on another sheet resting on top of this one, leaving grooves behind.

Valen tilted the lamp lower.

Now the grooves cast tiny shadows.

“Upper wall,” they read. “Two posts. Water gate.”

Sova looked at the first note.

“Same record?”

“Could be.”

“Same year?”

Valen paused.

“Could be.”

“Same handwriting?”

They moved the lantern again.

The surviving ink slanted right. The pressure marks beneath it were straighter.

“Probably not.”

By midday they had four partial readings.

**A — surviving ink.**

**B — pressure marks visible from the left.**

**C — pressure marks visible from below.**

**D — a pale stain that might once have held another line, or might simply be water damage.**

Valen disliked D.

It looked like it ought to mean something.

That was not the same as meaning something.

Sova brought out two nearby maintenance sheets from the same chest. One had a date. The other had none. Valen compared the handwriting, letter shapes and spacing.

“Reading A looks more like this dated sheet,” they said.

“Enough to call it the same hand?”

Valen leaned closer.

“No. Enough to say they resemble each other.”

Sova wrote that exact phrase instead.

Valen frowned at her note.

“You enjoy making certainty smaller.”

“I enjoy making notes survive questions.”

A worker from the water crew came to see the scroll.

“That is the old west-channel repair plan,” she said immediately.

Valen looked at her.

“How do you know?”

“My grandfather talked about it.”

“Which part?”

She pointed to a shape near the lower edge. “That bend.”

Under direct light, the shape looked like a curved channel.

Under raking light, a pressure line crossed through it and made the curve look like two unrelated marks.

Valen turned the sheet ninety degrees.

The worker frowned.

“Now it looks wrong.”

“Or it looked familiar before.”

She considered that.

“Both could be true.”

Instead of arguing, they asked her to describe what her grandfather had actually said. She remembered a repair near the west channel involving three old posts and a water gate, but she could not remember whether those details belonged to one repair or several stories told together.

Valen wrote her account on a separate page.

“Not on the transcription?” she asked.

“It is evidence about the scroll,” Valen said. “It is not writing that appears on the scroll.”

She nodded slowly. “Good distinction.”

They carried the four readings to the current west channel.

One old wall still had three post holes.

That supported reading A.

The upper wall had two repaired sockets beside a water gate.

That supported reading B.

Both readings matched real structures.

They could not both be one simple map of one moment.

Valen smiled despite themself.

The scroll was becoming less satisfying and more useful.

They measured the distance between the lower wall and the water gate. The proportions did not match the apparent curve on the page if the sheet were treated as a single scaled plan. Sova suggested the marks might be notes rather than a map. The water worker suggested the sheet might have been reused.

Valen liked both ideas.

They wrote neither as fact.

Back in the archive, Valen made a new label:

**At least two recording events may be present. Light angle reveals different physical traces. No single orientation is authoritative.**

Sova read it.

“Good.”

Valen looked at the pale stain.

“And that?”

“What do you think?”

“I think I want it to be another sentence.”

“That was not the question.”

Valen laughed.

They wrote:

**Unresolved mark. Do not transcribe as text without further evidence.**

The next morning, Valen tried one more thing. They carried the scroll to the north window and used daylight instead of the lantern. The stain became easier to see but no more legible. One mark that had looked like a letter under lamplight proved to be a wrinkle in the fibre.

Valen crossed one hopeful transcription from the notes.

Sova watched.

“Disappointed?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

Valen gave her a look.

“Not because you are disappointed. Because the record is allowed to become less exciting when the evidence gets better.”

That, Valen admitted, was fair.

Before storing the scroll, Sova asked another archive worker, Pela, to try the viewing methods without first reading Valen's conclusions. Pela found the surviving ink easily. She found some of the pressure grooves, missed others, and pointed to one line Valen had not recorded at all.

Valen leaned over the sheet.

“I thought that was fibre damage.”

“So did I at first,” Pela said. “It repeats when the lamp moves, though.”

They checked from two more angles. The line remained visible as a shallow groove.

Valen added it to the pressure-mark notes with a question mark rather than deciding what word it formed.

“Your method found something you missed,” Sova said.

Valen nodded.

“And another reader did not have to agree with your first reading to use it.”

That pleased them more than being right alone would have.

They made a simple viewing card for future readers: direct light for surviving ink, low side light for pressure marks, daylight for fibre damage. Beside each method they wrote what it could reveal and what it could not prove.

Then they rolled the scroll around a clean support and stored the reading notes, the worker's recollection and the viewing card beside it.

The lantern had revealed things.

It had not told them which story to believe.

That part still belonged to evidence, comparison and people willing to say when the page did not answer.
